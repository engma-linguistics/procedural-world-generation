# Trade, literature review round 1 (PW-1498 phase 1, lane 211)

Who trades what with whom, how far, carried by whom, in which eras, and what it changed. The design that uses this is
[design1.md](design1.md).

**How it was read.** Five reading agents worked in parallel on 2026-11-07, about 340 fetches in all. Every source cited below was
fetched and read, at least in the part cited. Marks on a citation:
- **(WP)**: Wikipedia, used as a pointer or for well-known facts;
- **(abs.)**: only the abstract or a landing page was read;
- **(sec.)**: a secondary or popular page reporting a primary source;
- **(snippet, unverified)**: seen only in a search result. Such a number is never used as a model anchor.

Books that could not be read are listed at the end. No number here was taken from memory.

## In plain words

- **Every kind of society trades, but not the same way.**
  - Forager bands give gifts to partners they know: on average 15 partners each, living up to ~200 km away. The gifts are an
    insurance policy for bad years.
  - Goods also pass from hand to hand down a chain. Obsidian is common within ~300 km of its source and then thins out steadily
    with distance.
  - Foragers next to farmers swap meat, honey and labour for starch, iron and salt. How much farm food a forager camp eats falls
    with how many hours' walk it is from the village.
  - Herders cannot live on their herds alone. They always need grain and crafts, and they drive horses to frontier markets by the
    tens of thousands a year. When trade is refused they take by raiding or tribute.
  - With towns come markets and specialist merchants. With states come caravans, ships, merchant colonies abroad, tolls, coined
    money, and tribute dressed up as trade.
- **Distance is everything, and water beats land.**
  - Carrying a tonne by land costs roughly 10 times as much as by river, and 25-35 times as much as by sea.
  - Grain loses about half its value in ~150 km of cart haulage, so bulk food goes far only by water.
  - Light, valuable goods (tin, textiles, silver, gold, spice, gems, beads) go thousands of km by land.
  - Trade between Bronze Age towns fell with distance faster than trade does today (gravity elasticity ~1.9, against ~1 for
    modern trade).
- **Salt is the universal want.** Farmers' diets, herders' animals and preserved fish all need it. It was boiled and traded as
  ingots by 5600 BC at Provadia, carried across the Sahara two slabs to a camel, and swapped against herring in the Hanseatic
  Baltic.
- **What trade changed.** It made insurance networks (hxaro) and patron-client ties (forager-farmer). It made nomads and states
  mutually dependent. It founded merchant colonies (Kanesh, Uruk outposts) and rich trading towns. It moved faiths and writing:
  Islam and literacy travelled with the Saharan caravans. And it made whole systems fragile to the loss of one link (the Late
  Bronze Age collapse).

## 1. Foragers: gifts, chains and circuits

**Hxaro** (Ju/'hoansi San; Wiessner & Huang 2022, PNAS, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9564820):
- In 1974 at /Kae/kae, adults kept on average 15 partnerships, living 2-200 km away; ~70% of the partners were across the border
  in Namibia.
- People spent on average 4.4 months a year living with partners, and 69% of possessions came as hxaro gifts. Beadwork was the
  valued gift; tools were also given.
- Gifts travelled along chains of partners. In hard times families dispersed along hxaro links. It worked because the region
  varied enough in rain and people for risk pooling.
- It faded with settlement, wild food running out near villages, and cash: partners halved by 1997, and distant or non-kin gifting
  fell from 23% to 1% by 2018.
- Pearce & Moutsiou 2014 (J. Hum. Evol., https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4157217) report each !Kung family with at least
  one partner ~150-200 km away.
- The bead network is old. Strontium in ostrich-eggshell beads from Lesotho shelters shows beads from >100 km and >300 km, over
  ≥33 kyr (Stewart et al. 2020, via Science News (sec.) https://sciencenews.org/?p=3085849; minimum ~330 km per the Cambridge
  Archaeological Journal text).

**Reciprocity and social distance.** Sahlins (1972) puts exchange on one scale of social distance:
- generalized reciprocity (gifts with no reckoning) among close kin;
- balanced reciprocity (like for like) in the community;
- negative reciprocity (haggling, up to theft) with strangers.

This is read through the Mapping Archaeological Aspects course text (sec.), https://mapaspects.org/book/export/html/1865/index.html,
and Windler 2013 (Metalla 20.2). Windler adds that strangers need a neutral third party to enforce terms. That is the opening for
markets, merchants and ports of trade (§5).

**Down-the-line and fall-off** (Renfrew 1969/1977, via the Mapping Archaeological Aspects text (sec.),
https://mapaspects.org/book/export/html/1901/index.html):
- Renfrew's zones:
  - the supply zone is within ~300 km of an obsidian source, where obsidian is over 80% of the chipped stone;
  - beyond it is a contact zone, where the share falls off with distance;
  - the interaction zone is where ≥30% of the obsidian comes from one source.
- The zone thresholds have no clear ethnographic basis, and random walks give similar curves. So **fall-off curves separate
  high-value goods from bulky ones, but not reciprocity from markets**.
- An agent-based network study of Anatolian obsidian (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4590507):
  - obsidian reached southern Levant sites 700-800 km from its source, at ~0.5% of the flint;
  - pure village-to-village relay at 50% kept per hop leaves 0.39% after 8 hops, which needs villages ~100 km apart;
  - models with some distant links (120-300 km) fit better.
- The Renfrew, Dixon & Cann papers themselves were not read (extract only).

**Expeditions and axes (Australia).**
- Pituri: Dieri men walked ~400 km a year to the Herbert River pituri country, ~32 kg (70 lb) each. Pituri gained value with
  distance and was haggled. Gatherings of 500+ people are recorded. Source: Gason 1882 via Odyssey Traveller (sec.),
  https://odysseytraveller.com/articles/how-was-pituri-traded-in-ancient-aboriginal-australia.
- Mount William greenstone axes are found up to 800 km from the quarry, but not in eastern Victoria, where the Gunaikurnai had
  their own quarries: a boundary that may have lasted millennia. Source: Victorian government fact sheet,
  https://firstpeoplesrelations.vic.gov.au/fact-sheet-aboriginal-ground-edge-axes.

**Kula** (Malinowski 1922, Argonauts of the Western Pacific, https://biblioteca.scriptorium.ro/malinowski/argonauts_of_the_western_pacific/):
- Shell necklaces (2-5 m strings of red discs) and armshells circulate among the Massim islands. About 90% of armshells are too small
  to wear: the objects are "not owned in order to be used".
- Tubetube-Murua is 120-135 miles, four nights' sail. Tubetube are middlemen who also run non-Kula trade.
- The Trobrianders keep ceremonial gift exchange apart from barter for commodities, gimwali (HRAF teaching page,
  https://hraf.yale.edu/teach-ehraf/reciprocity-exchange-the-kula-ring).
- A replica Kula canoe sailed 225 km in three legs in 2002 (Dudley et al., Pacific Archaeology).

**How far forager goods go** (Pearce & Moutsiou 2014):
- Maximum obsidian transfers: ~200 km in low-latitude Africa, ~300 km for Neanderthals, >400 km for Upper Palaeolithic humans.
  Shells reached ~800 km in the European Upper Palaeolithic.
- Binford's median territory radii: dispersed band 9-24 km, tribe 61-146 km.
- Transfers show contact potential, not proof of exchange.
- The oldest hint: Olorgesailie (Kenya), ~300 ka, obsidian from up to ~95 km while handaxe stone came from within 5 km
  (Brooks et al. via Springer "Behind the Paper" (sec.); the Science paper itself returned 403).

## 2. Forager-farmer and herder-farmer exchange

**Congo Basin.** Kitanishi 1995, African Study Monographs 16(2): 73-118, open, read in full.
- **What moves.** Foragers give meat, forest goods and farm labour. Villagers give cassava and plantain, salt, cigarettes,
  clothes and ironwork.
- **How much (his Table 11, compiling Ichikawa, Bailey & Peacock and Sawada).** Farm food as a share of forager energy:
  - Mbuti: 63% in the dry season, 11% in the honey season;
  - Efe: 34-41% (plus 10% oil palm);
  - Aka of his study: 11% (plus 11% oil palm), the least dependent group;
  - other groups: "about 50%";
  - Lobaye Aka: 55% by meal frequency (Bahuchet).
- **Distance.** Dependence "showed inverse correlation to the distance from the village to the camp". A camp 1.5 h from the village
  ate much farm food; camps 4-5 h away did not. A food basket weighs 20-30 kg.
- **Barter.** One fur plus ~15 kg of meat bought 1.35 kg of plantain and 13 kg of cassava, closer to a gift than balanced exchange.
- **Efe and Lese** (Cultural Survival web page, https://www.culturalsurvival.org/node/4822, authors given only in part):
  - women's labour buys the cultivated food that is the "mainstay" of the diet;
  - ties are inherited between a band and a village partner;
  - camps are mostly within 4 h of the village;
  - the relationship is unequal.

The ladder's existing cap (`ladder.BARTER_CAP` 0.5) is consistent with these numbers, and the distance relation is now sourced
in shape. That bears on PW-1405.

**Agta and Palanan farmers, Luzon.** Peterson 1977, in Hutterer (ed.), Economic Exchange and Social Interaction in Southeast
Asia, pp. 55-65, open access (https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/41852/1/9780472901722.pdf).
- **Who.** About 800 Agta hunt and fish beside peasant farmers.
- **Dependence both ways.** "Nearly all Agta obtain 70 to 100 percent of their carbohydrate foods from Palanan." About two-thirds
  of the peasants get 30-50%+ of their protein from the Agta.
- **Partnerships.** Special-friend partnerships (ibay) bind nearly all Agta and a third of the peasants, with credit. Farmers
  plant 10-30% more than they need, against Agta requests.
- **The rate varies by place.** The fish:corn trade ratio runs 0.21 in the north, 0.36 inland and 1.28 near the town: a sixfold
  spread in one region. An exchange rate is not a constant.

**Kalahari.** Lee & Guenther 1993, History in Africa 20 (abs.). This is the Kalahari debate: were the San an underclass of
Bantu-speaking herders, or independent but in contact? Histories varied. Clientage, with service and raiding, is a mode beside
free trade. No quantities were read.

**Steppe and sown.**
- Khazanov 2019, "Steppe nomads in the Eurasian trade", Chungara 51(1): 85-93, open
  (https://www.chungara.cl/Vols/2019/51-1/007-KHAZANOV_CON_DOI.pdf):
  - the nomadic economy is "specialized and non-autarchic", always needing farm and craft products;
  - regional trade with settled neighbours mattered most;
  - nomads drove their own herds to frontier trade centres;
  - Russia bought **20,000-80,000 horses a year from the Nogai** (16th c.), "limits set not by demand but by supply";
  - the Uyghur extracted **40 pieces of silk per horse** from Tang China;
  - the Xiongnu always insisted on border markets in treaties;
  - nomad society had no professional traders: foreign merchants (Bukharan, Sogdian) did the carrying.
  - (Khazanov's 1984 book was not read.)
- Barfield's "outer frontier strategy", read via Hall 2010 (Cliodynamics 1(1), review (sec.)): nomads raid and demand tribute,
  not territory. Steppe confederacies and Chinese dynasties rose and fell together.
- **Tea and horses.** Yang Fuquan, The Silk Road journal (https://edspace.american.edu/silkroadjournal/):
  - the Song Tea and Horse Office opened in 1074, and "more than 20,000 warhorses per year" were exchanged for tea;
  - at least half of Sichuan's 15,000 t of tea a year went to Tibet;
  - the Sichuan-Lhasa road is ~2,350 km in 56 stages.
- **Maasai and Kikuyu** (Kawa Digital Books (sec., weak)): hides, livestock, salt and beads against pots, yams, tobacco and cereals.
  Women did most of the trading. Herders who lost their herds fell back on farming.
- **Bedouin** (Library of Congress country study, https://countrystudies.us/saudi-arabia/24.htm): "Nomadism has never been a
  self-contained system." Dates and grain came from the oases. Khuwa was a town's payment to nomads for peace.

**Salt and herds.**
- A sheep needs ~9 g of salt a day (MSD Veterinary Manual).
- Tuareg salt caravans to Bilma (Bradshaw Foundation (sec.), https://bradshawfoundation.com/africa/tuareg_salt_caravans/index.php):
  - camels carry ~300 kg and cover ~50 km/day in the dunes; past caravans exceeded 20,000 camels;
  - millet, cheese and goats go out, and salt pillars and dates come back, at two measures of millet for three of dates;
  - caravans leave in autumn and return in spring.

## 3. Early farming and the Bronze Age

**Neolithic.**
- **Provadia-Solnitsata, Bulgaria** (project site (sec.), https://www.provadia-solnitsata.com/en/about):
  - brine was boiled from ~5600 BC; yields rose from ~25 kg to 300-500 kg a batch, and up to ~5 t a cycle by 4700-4450 BC;
  - salt ingots of 20 g to 8 kg were a medium of exchange;
  - salt reached the Aegean, from a settlement of only ~0.5 ha.
- **Alpine jade axes** (Delgado-Raack, Risch et al. 2019, via UAB press release (sec.)): most polished axes moved <200 km, rarely
  400-500 km. Only Alpine jadeitite went >1,000 km, valued for labour and quality, not cost-efficiently.
- **Spondylus shell** went from the Aegean into central Europe, 5350-4200 BC ((WP) and Windler 2013).

**Copper and tin.** Pulak 2008, "The Uluburun Shipwreck and Late Bronze Age Trade", in Beyond Babylon
(https://cgs.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/27/2020/12/Pulak-2008-Reading.pdf):
- **Cargo.** The ship sank ~1300 BC carrying ~10 t of Cypriot copper in 354 ingots (~24 kg each) and ~1 t of tin (originally more).
  That is the 10:1 bronze ratio, 11 t of bronze in all. The ship was 15 m long and carried ≥20 t. Other cargo: glass ingots, resin,
  ebony, ivory and ~70,000 beads.
- **Where the tin came from** is disputed:
  - Central Asia per Powell et al. 2022 (press coverage (sec.));
  - SW Britain more likely per Berger et al. 2023, Frontiers in Earth Science, open
    (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1211478/full).
- **The point for the model.** Tin is rare at the source and needed everywhere bronze is made, so it moves 1,000-4,000+ km. Cline
  likens it to oil today (Long Now talk 2016 (sec.)).

**Uruk expansion** (3600-3100 BC). Algaze himself was not read: paywalled, with the U. Chicago Press blurb (abs.) and an anonymous
critical essay (sec.) used instead.
- Lowland Mesopotamia was rich in food and transport but short of stone, hardwood and metal, so it planted outposts up the
  Euphrates. Habuba Kabira's walled enclaves were 20-40 ha and held ~5,000 people.
- The essay doubts it was purely commercial.

**Old Assyrian trade, Assur to Kanesh.** Main sources: Dercksen 2022, in Warburton (ed.), The Earliest Economic Growth in World
History, pp. 75-104, open (https://hdl.handle.net/1887/3721453); Barjamovic, Chaney, Coşar & Hortaçsu 2019, QJE 134(3)
(https://keremcosar.uvacreate.virginia.edu/publications/BCCH-BronzeAgeTrade-qje.pdf).
- **Route.** ~1,200 km, 6-7 weeks, twice a year; the Taurus passes are shut in winter (Alexander & Violet (sec.), citing Veenhof).
- **Loads.** A donkey carried ~65 kg of tin (2 talents 10 minas) or ~25 textiles. Caravans ran 10-50 donkeys, up to 348. One
  driver handled 2-3 donkeys.
- **Volume.** Estimates differ:
  - Larsen: ≥4 t of tin and ~3,800 textiles a year, "could be doubled";
  - Barjamovic: 15 t of tin and 32,000 textiles;
  - Dercksen: ≥1,000 donkey-loads a year.
- **Profit and money.** Net profit ~50% (Dercksen), up to 100% per round trip (Veenhof via (sec.)); loans at 30% a year.
- **Tolls and duties.** Road tolls ~10% of value; the palace's duty 1/20 of textiles and 2/65 of tin.
- **People abroad.** About 30 colonies and stations; at least 700-800 Assyrians in Anatolia; family firms (father in Assur, sons in
  Kanesh).
- **The gravity fit.** Trade fell as distance^-1.9 (ζ = 1.912, s.e. 0.19, QJE) against ~1 for modern trade. A city's hinterland
  is taken as 30 km (a day's walk), and the mean spacing of cities was 281 km.

**Indus, Gulf and Mesopotamia** (WP and Dayalan 2018 (sec.)): carnelian beads at Ur from ~2600 BC; ships of Meluhha, Magan and
Dilmun at Akkad (Sargon); timber, carnelian, ivory and copper; Indus-type weights in Mesopotamia. No tonnages were read.

**The Late Bronze Age collapse.** Knapp & Manning 2016, AJA 120(1), open (https://ajaonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/1201_Knapp.pdf):
- no single cause; candidate causes are many;
- polities had "become all too closely interdependent", and "it would be folly to deny the relevance of a deep-seated decline in
  international trade";
- piracy sped the end;
- independent traders may have buffered Cyprus.

## 4. Long-distance caravan and maritime trade

**Silk Roads** (WP for the outline; Hansen, Turfan oasis paper, Yale, https://history.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/hansen-silk-road-trade.pdf):
- Goods changed hands many times, and few merchants went end to end.
- Sogdians were the long-haul middlemen:
  - 41 of 49 merchants in one Turfan document bore Sogdian names;
  - one 7th-century partnership ran ~2,500 km west of Chang'an with 2 camels, 4 cattle and a donkey, on a loan of 275 bolts of silk.
- Silk Road caravan sizes and camel loads: **unsourced** in this round.

**Indian Ocean**:
- The Periplus (1st c. AD) lists the goods: wine, copper, tin and coin into India; pepper, pearls, ivory, textiles and gems out ((WP),
  World History Encyclopedia (sec.)).
- Pepper was ~3/4 of the cargo bulk from Muziris. Ships sailed July-September on the SW monsoon and returned on the NE (Dec-Jan);
  the round trip took ~a year (Martinez 2017, https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/IndOc-Gulf/IndianOcean-Martinez2017.pdf).
- The Muziris papyrus cargo (the Hermapollon): ~150 t worth ~9 million sesterces, with import tax over 2 million. Strabo counted
  120 ships a year. Source: Cobb 2023 via NYRB review (sec.).
- Austronesian sailors reached East Africa and Madagascar by the end of the 1st millennium BC (Beaujard (abs.)).
- Spice markups from the Moluccas to Europe: **unverified** this round.

**Trans-Saharan**:
- **Ibn Battuta**, Rihla, 1352 (translated excerpts, https://faculty.tnstate.edu/tcorse/h120/ibn_battuta.htm):
  - Taghaza's houses were built of salt blocks, and "a camel will carry two of these slabs";
  - a load sold for 8-10 mithqals of gold at Walata and 20-30 (up to 40) at Mali, and salt was cut and used as money;
  - Sijilmasa to Mali took ~4.5 months with stops.
- **Caravans** (OER Project (sec.)): 5,000-10,000 camels were common by the 13th c.; camels were rented from pastoralists and
  carried ~180 kg (400 lb); about 20 miles a day and ~70 days to cross, October to March.
- **Rule and faith** ((WP)): the Almoravids took Aoudaghost c. 1050; Islam reached West African ruling elites by ~1200, along the
  routes.

**Mediterranean** ((WP), Cura Annonae; Monte Testaccio):
- Rome imported 150,000-272,000 t of grain a year (Erdkamp, Rickman), Africa feeding it 8 months and Egypt 4. Ships averaged ~70 t,
  making 2,000-3,000 voyages a year.
- Monte Testaccio holds ~53 million oil amphorae: ~130,000 a year at its peak, AD 140-250.

**Lapita** ((WP); Specht via ANU Press ch. 32): sites span >4,000 km, 1600-500 BC. Watom obsidian came ~270 km from Talasea.
Claims of 3,400 km to Fiji are snippets only.

**Hanseatic League** (Uni-Trier EGO, https://ego-plone.uni-trier.de/resolveuid/3c9d9db62bec4d30b359b1e186540e6e; History of the
Germans (sec.)):
- **Kontors** at Novgorod, Bergen, London and Bruges.
- **Goods:** herring and grain for salt; Bergen stockfish for grain; cloth for furs and wax.
- **Volumes:** 550 ships brought 65,000 barrels of herring to Lübeck in 1400; the Scania fair handled 200,000-300,000 barrels a year
  at its peak.
- **Salt** came from Lüneburg against bay salt from Bourgneuf.

**Salt generally** ((WP), Salt trade): Wieliczka produced ~40,000 t a year at its peak; the Via Salaria ran ~242 km.

## 5. Institutions

**Markets and central places.**
- Christaller's marketing principle is k = 3 (Transport Geography, https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter2/transport-and-spatial-organization/central-places-theory-variations/).
- **Skinner** (Corbett & Rebich 2004, CSISS, https://escholarship.org/content/qt51x4g3qh/qt51x4g3qh.pdf; Skinner 1964-65, JAS 24,
  footnotes via the Cambridge pages):
  - a standard market town serves ~18 villages in two rings;
  - periodic markets meet on a 10-day cycle, staggered between levels;
  - Chengdu-plain standard areas average 41 km² and 11,558 people; a mountain county's 76 km² and 7,659.
  - **Skinner rejects walking distance as what sets a market area's size:** most are smaller than a day's walk requires, and on the
    periphery bigger.
- So a market's reach in the model should come from settled density, not a fixed walking radius.

**Money.**
- **Coinage** (Kroll, in Lydians and their World, https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/latw-kroll-coins-of-sardis):
  - electrum coins came in the 7th c. BC in Asia Minor; the Lydian state over-valued them by 15-20%;
  - denominations went down to 1/96 of a stater (1/24 could buy a sheep or a bushel of grain);
  - Croesus's pure gold and silver coins "travelled widely".
- **Before coins:** barley and then silver by weight were money in Mesopotamia (snippet, unverified).
- Salt ingots at Provadia and salt pieces in Mali served as money (§3, §4).

**Merchant diasporas** (Curtin 1984, read via Barraclough's 1985 NYRB review (sec.)):
- Foreign merchant communities living in host towns are the normal institution of trade between cultures: the Aro, Bugis,
  Armenians, the Steelyard in London, and a merchants' town beside the king's in ancient Ghana.
- The Old Assyrians in Kanesh are an early case (§3).
- Curtin also stresses that the local Sahel herder-grain trade outweighed long-distance trade early on.
- The claim that diasporas "work themselves out of business" was not confirmed (snippet).

**Polanyi** (WP for the scheme; "Ports of trade in early societies", J. Econ. Hist. 1963 (abs.)):
- Three forms: reciprocity, redistribution and market exchange. They coexist.
- A port of trade (Whydah, Aztec and Maya enclaves) separates administered long-distance trade from local markets.
- Critiques: West African research discredited much of the substantivist reading.
- Model reading: use the three as labels on flows, not as stages a society passes through.

**Tribute against trade** ((WP) pages):
- Ming tribute missions brought back gifts and trading rights worth more than the tribute, so states sent as many as allowed. Ryukyu
  sent 57 in 1372-98.
- The Inca had little money, storehouses along the roads, and Murra's "vertical archipelago" of redistribution (contested).
- Aztec pochteca sold surplus tribute and went ahead of conquest.

## 6. Costs: what bears distance

**Mode costs.** Scheidel, Meeks & Weiland 2012, ORBIS v1 paper (https://orbis.stanford.edu/orbis2012/ORBIS_v1paper_20120501.pdf),
from Diocletian's Price Edict (AD 301):
- per kg of wheat per km: donkey or camel 0.028 denarii, wagon 0.035, river 0.0034 downstream and 0.0068 upstream, sea ~0.001 (at
  ~100 km/day);
- so **sea : river down : river up : pack : wagon ≈ 1 : 3.4 : 6.8 : 28 : 35** (lane arithmetic from ORBIS's numbers);
- published land:river:sea ratios from Duncan-Jones (1 : 4.9 : 56) and others were seen only in a snippet.

| Mode | Load | km/day | Cost per t-km, sea = 1 | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sea | ships, 70 t average (Rome) | ~100 | 1 | ORBIS; annona (WP) |
| river downstream | barges | 65 (45-75) | ~3.4 | ORBIS |
| river upstream | barges | 15 (10-30) | ~6.8 | ORBIS |
| pack donkey | ~65-100 kg | 24-30 | ~28 | ORBIS; Dercksen (65 kg tin) |
| camel | ~180-300 kg | 30-50 | ~28 | ORBIS (194 kg Edict load); OER; Bradshaw |
| wagon / cart | ~400 kg | 30 (ox cart 12) | ~35 | ORBIS |
| llama | 25-35 kg | 15-20 | n/a | Tripcevich dissertation §3.2.5 (11 sources) |
| porter | 20-25 kg (forager women 20-30) | 20-25 | China: ~75x river | ORBIS speed; Tlamemeh (WP); Cheng 2024 |

**Two other periods agree on the order:**
- Late-17th-century China (Cheng 2024, LSE WP 371, https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/information/wp371.pdf): river 0.016-0.043,
  wheeled cart 0.077, pack mule on the flat 0.213, in hills 0.452, porter 1.594 taels per 100 kg per 100 km. That makes **river :
  pack ≈ 1 : 10**, and porter ≈ 75x river.
- England about 1700 (Alvarez-Palau, Bogart et al. 2022, https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~dbogart/Multimodalmodel16801830nov162022.pdf):
  river 1 d per ton-mile against wagon 10.6 and packhorse 11.9, so **≈ 1 : 11**.

**What bears distance:**
- Cato: moving a mill 75 miles cost 39% of its value (Adams 2007, Oxford working paper).
- "Cheaper to ship grain from one end of the Mediterranean to the other than to cart it 75 miles" (Jones, quoted by Adams).
- Land carriage of wheat over 100 Roman miles costs ~55% of its value, against 1.3% by sea (Duncan-Jones via a SJSU page; snippet).
- So **bulk grain goes ~100-200 km overland at most and thousands of km by water**.
- Tin, textiles, silver, salt slabs on camels, pepper and beads carry far. The Kanesh caravans carried only high-value goods.
- Value per weight is the switch. Fall-off curves (§1) agree: they separate valuable goods from bulky ones.

**Gravity.** Bronze Age trade fell with distance as d^-1.9 (Barjamovic et al. 2019).

## What the model takes from this (feeds design1.md)

1. **Goods and wants come from the land and the way of life**, not from tuned numbers:
   - farmers lack salt;
   - herders lack grain ("non-autarchic", Khazanov);
   - bronze users lack tin;
   - foragers near farmers lack starch;
   - towns lack grain and fuel.
2. **One cost graph with mode ratios from §6**: sea 1, river ~3-7, pack and cart ~28-35 (China and England give land:river ~10-11).
   The modes open with the techs the tree already has.
3. **One fall-off law**, exponential in cost as in Renfrew's curves, with a **decay length per value class**:
   - bulk: about the 100-200 km of overland grain;
   - middling: about the ~300 km supply zone of obsidian and the existing 450 cost-km of salt;
   - precious: 1,000+ km, as for tin and Kanesh.
   The cost (not the km) carries the era, because the cost falls as boats, pack animals and roads arrive.
4. **The kind of exchange is a label from the two societies** (gift, barter, market, merchant, tribute). Fall-off curves can't tell
   them apart, so the label does not change the physics. It changes who gets the goods (a town's market, a polity's seat) and what
   the brief says.
5. **What trade does**, each with a source: insurance between foragers; dependence and clientage between foragers or herders and
   farmers; raids and tribute when markets close; merchant colonies and quarters abroad; wealth to towns on routes; faith and writing
   along routes; fragility when a link breaks.
