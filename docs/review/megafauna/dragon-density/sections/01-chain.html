2. The chain, step by step

Each step takes the one before it as input. The numbers are the model's own constants. Where the literature says a step is weak, the step says so here, and Section 9 says what should change.

  1. Plants to grazers. The world's grazing net primary production (NPP, in g C/m²/yr: the herb layer's NPP plus 30% of the shrubs' as browse, cut by up to 60% where the ground is frozen or snowed in) becomes standing herbivore biomass through McNaughton et al.'s fit, log10 B = 1.52 log10 NAP − 4.79, with B in kJ/m² and NAP in kJ/m²/yr (51 data points, r² = 0.579; McNaughton et al. 1989, p. 143). The slope is "significantly greater than 1" (p. 143), so a larger input gives a disproportionately larger herd. Two mismatches matter. First, McNaughton's NAP is above-ground production only: "Only processes occurring above ground were included" (p. 142; Fig. 1 caption, "net above-ground primary productivity"). The model's NPP is whole-plant, fine roots included, so on open grassland its input is larger than McNaughton's NAP; an input twice the true NAP gives 21.52 = 2.9 times the biomass. This is an upward bias of unknown size where grass dominates (Section 3 brackets it). Second, McNaughton's B is the whole herbivore level: the data were kept only if "the preponderance of the herbivore level was accounted for" (p. 142), and the paper finds "dominance by heterotherm herbivores in forests and homeotherms in grasslands" (p. 143), while the model reads it as big game. The paper turned masses into energy "using standard conversion factors" without giving them (pp. 142–143; it cites Golley 1961, Schroeder 1977 and Whitfield 1977, none consulted here), so the model's unit factors are its own: 0.45 g C per g dry plant and 17.5 kJ per g dry plant (both unsourced), and 7 kJ per g of live animal, which is Farlow's figure (Farlow 1993, p. 172). With these, a hot desert's median 90 g C/m²/yr holds {{ sig(N['prey_anchor']['90'],2) }} t/km² of grazers, a steppe's 200 g C/m²/yr holds {{ sig(N['prey_anchor']['200'],2) }} t/km², and a rich savanna's 470 g C/m²/yr holds {{ sig(N['prey_anchor']['470'],2) }} t/km². Coe, Cumming & Phillipson's fit for twelve semi-arid African wildlife areas (rainfall under 700 mm a year), log10 B = 1.094 log10 NAAP + 0.389 (r = 0.966; B large-mammal herbivores in kg/km² live weight; NAAP above-ground production in g dry/m²/yr, predicted from rainfall by Rosenzweig's 1968 formula rather than measured; Coe et al. 1976, Fig. 3, p. 349, and p. 347), gives 0.8, 1.9 and 4.9 t/km² for the same three places, the last at the top edge of its data. The two agree within a factor of 1.5. Coe's line is for large mammals only and only for semi-arid Africa; the model keeps McNaughton's because it spans forests and tundra too.
  2. How much can be eaten a year. The model lets the whole predator guild, the peoples included, take 10% of the standing wild biomass a year, plus 10% of the herders' stock. Neither figure has a source. Three papers bound the first. Farlow gives prey turnover as 0.94 M−0.28 a year (M in kg; Farlow 1993, eq. 6, p. 172): {{ sig(N['literature']['farlow_turnover']['100'],2) }}, {{ sig(N['literature']['farlow_turnover']['500'],2) }} and {{ sig(N['literature']['farlow_turnover']['1000'],2) }} a year for 100, 500 and 1,000 kg grazers. Coe et al. put large African herbivores' production at {{ sig(N['sensitivity']['coe_pb']['min'],2) }} to {{ sig(N['sensitivity']['coe_pb']['max'],2) }} of their biomass a year across 24 wildlife areas (median {{ sig(N['sensitivity']['coe_pb']['median'],2) }}; Table 1, pp. 344–345), from assumed production-to-biomass ratios of 0.05, 0.20 and 0.35 for large, intermediate and small herbivores (pp. 343–344). McNaughton et al.'s own net secondary production fit, log10 NSP = 1.10 log10 NAP − 3.27 (r² = 0.364, 35 points; p. 143), set against their biomass fit, gives {{ sig(N['mcnaughton_nsp']['90']['NSP_over_B'],2) }}, {{ sig(N['mcnaughton_nsp']['200']['NSP_over_B'],2) }} and {{ sig(N['mcnaughton_nsp']['470']['NSP_over_B'],2) }} times the standing biomass a year at the three places above (our arithmetic: a ratio of two fits to different data, both counting the whole herbivore level, cold-blooded herbivores (mostly invertebrates, on our reading) included, so a weak bound for big game). So 10% a year is about half of what large herds produce: cautious, but not far inside. It is flat: the model does not vary it with prey size, though small grazers turn over faster than large ones.
  3. The peoples eat first. Each people's hunters take its density × 2,200 kcal a day × the share of its food that is wild game: 50% of a forager's food, 5% of anyone else's, blended by how much of a people's living is foraging (a people half foragers and half farmers takes 27.5%). A head of game holds 625,000 edible kcal per 0.5 t. That is {{ sig(N['chain']['forager_game_t_yr'],2) }} t of live prey a year per pure forager and {{ sig(N['chain']['other_game_t_yr'],2) }} t per farmer or herder. All three numbers are unsourced. (The dragons' side counts the same 0.5 t head as 836,000 kcal, of which 85% is assimilated: about 711,000 kcal. One animal, two values; the difference is about 14% of the peoples' take.) The sum is taken over a dragon's 60 km reach and capped at the wild supply.
  4. Then the giants, at their own densities and a food need scaled from body mass. They take {{ pct(min(era('on','0',c,'shares')['giants'] for c in ('none','foragers','herders','farmers','others')),1) }} to {{ pct(max(era('on','0',c,'shares')['giants'] for c in ('none','foragers','herders','farmers','others')),1) }} of the supply, by kind of country (Figure 3).
  5. Then the predator guild, split by size. Carbone, Turvey & Bielby list the carnivorous theropods of Late Cretaceous North America in four size classes, with their predicted shares of the guild's individuals: 20.6 kg (79.9%), 58.3 kg (19.0%), 1,123 kg (0.9%) and 5,347 kg (0.1%) (Carbone et al. 2011, Table 1, p. 2683). Those shares come from Carbone & Gittleman's mammalian carnivore density line, 1.97 M−0.88 per km² (p. 2685; their Fig. 2B line, 197.6 M−0.88 per 100 km²), summed over each class's species. They therefore reflect that fauna's count of species per class (five small species, one T. rex) as well as body size. The paper counts animals, not food. The model weights each class's head count by M0.75 to get its food. That weighting has a source: Carbone & Gittleman expect carnivore numbers per unit of prey production to scale as M−0.75 and find an exponent "not significantly different from −0.75" (fitted −0.66, confidence limits −0.48 to −0.85; Carbone & Gittleman 2002, p. 2274; note 10, p. 2275). The two classes of 1 t and up then eat {{ pct(N['chain']['dragon_share'],3) }} of the guild's food and the smaller classes the rest: about {{ sig(N['chain']['guild_ratio'],3) }} units for every unit the apex class eats. The dragons are that apex class. One Cretaceous guild stands in for every world's guild. Farlow makes the point that such a guild exists: a community with dinosaur-sized predators would also hold smaller predators "that could consume at least some of the prey productivity" (Farlow 1993, p. 176). His own correction for it is milder than the model's: moving from 50:1 to 100:1 halves the apex predator (p. 176), against the model's cut to {{ pct(N['chain']['dragon_share'],2) }}. Two choices here are open: the exponent (Section 3), and the fact that every dragon, wyrmlings included, eats from the apex class's share (Section 9).
  6. A dragon's food. The model gives every dragon kind four life stages, wyrmling, young, adult and ancient, and a default spread of ages across them: of the dragons alive, {{ pct(N['chain']['stage_share'][0],2) }} are wyrmlings (mean {{ sig(N['chain']['stage_mass_kg'][0],2) }} kg), {{ pct(N['chain']['stage_share'][1],2) }} young ({{ sig(N['chain']['stage_mass_kg'][1],2) }} kg), {{ pct(N['chain']['stage_share'][2],2) }} adults and {{ pct(N['chain']['stage_share'][3],2) }} ancients. Averaged over that spread, the dragon burns {{ sig(N['chain']['kcal_day'],3) }} kcal a day on the reptile field metabolic rate of Nagy, Girard & Brown, 0.196 M0.889 kJ/day with M in g (Nagy et al. 1999, Table 2, eq. 32, p. 261; all reptiles, n = 55). Eaten as whole 0.5 t carcasses of 836 Mcal each (Farlow's 7 kJ/g), with an unsourced 85% assimilated, that is {{ sig(N['chain']['cattle_yr'],3) }} head or {{ sig(N['chain']['food_t_yr'],2) }} t of live prey a year. This is an average over every age: it equals the rate of a single {{ sig(N['chain']['single_reptile_mass_for_mean_intake_kg']/1000,2) }} t reptile. A dragon at the 1.65 t ecological mass would kill {{ sig(N['chain']['kill_t_at_eco_mass'],2) }} t a year on the same law. Adults (mean {{ sig(N['chain']['stage_mass_kg'][2]/1000,2) }} t) and ancients (mean {{ sig(N['chain']['stage_mass_kg'][3]/1000,2) }} t, up to 52 t) eat {{ pct(N['chain']['stage_food_share'][2]+N['chain']['stage_food_share'][3],2) }} of the food. The fit's largest reptile is the 45.2 kg Komodo dragon (Table 1, p. 258), so that food is eaten at 60 to 1,100 times the heaviest animal in the data. Nagy et al. warn that outside the fitted masses the 95% interval "will be larger" (p. 262). Within the data a species' true rate can be "as low as 30% of predicted or as high as 170%" (Nagy 2005, p. 1622), and the food equations leave out the food for growth (p. 1624), which matters for an animal that grows all its life. The reptile slope (0.889) is steeper than the mammal slope (0.734, Nagy et al. 1999, Table 2, p. 260), so the extrapolation does not stay at a lizard's rate: it gives {{ pct(N['sensitivity']['fmr_at_eco_mass_kj']['reptile']/N['sensitivity']['fmr_at_eco_mass_kj']['mammal'],2) }} of a mammal's rate at 1.65 t. McNab finds varanid lizards at a flat 22% of mammals (1.07 M0.735 kJ/day, 2.2 to 45.2 kg; McNab 2009, p. 12185) and concludes the same ratio, 22% of mammals, for dinosaurs; he reports Barrick & Showers's estimate of "31% of mammals" as similar (p. 12187). In effect the model's dragons eat as a mesotherm would (an animal whose metabolic rate lies between a reptile's and a mammal's), somewhat above both dinosaur estimates and close to Marshall et al.'s "physiology midway between that of mammalian carnivores and that of large varanid lizards" (Marshall et al. 2021, p. 284), not as a lizard would. The ecological mass itself is Marshall et al.'s measure: the survivorship-weighted mean mass of the animals past their juvenile years, their "ecological body mass" (p. 284); under the default schedule it is {{ sig(N['chain']['eco_mass_kg']/1000,3) }} t.
  7. Dragons per km². Where nobody else eats, density = 10% × {{ pct(N['chain']['dragon_share'],3) }} × prey ÷ {{ sig(N['chain']['food_t_yr'],3) }} t. That is 1 dragon per {{ sig(N['chain']['one_per_km2_at_1t'],3) }} km² for each t/km² of prey: {{ per(N['chain']['per_km2_per_t']*N['prey_anchor']['90']) }} on a desert's {{ sig(N['prey_anchor']['90'],2) }} t/km², {{ per(N['chain']['per_km2_per_t']*N['prey_anchor']['200']) }} on a steppe's {{ sig(N['prey_anchor']['200'],2) }} t/km², and {{ per(N['chain']['per_km2_per_t']*N['prey_anchor']['470']) }} on a rich savanna's {{ sig(N['prey_anchor']['470'],2) }} t/km². Where peoples live, they reach the dragons through three channels. (1) Overkill: wild herds are cut by up to 60% where peoples live (outside mountains and tundra). The cut follows an overkill pressure, 1 − exp(−N/0.05) with N people per km², which already reaches {{ pct(N['literature']['pressure_at']['0.1'],2) }} at 0.1 people per km². (2) Hunting: the peoples' hunters take first from what is left (step 3). (3) Persecution: the peoples kill dragons, and the dragons breed back, by age class. A place's kill effort is {{ N['chain']['kill_q'] }} × the people per km² × their means. The means combine what a people has to defend (a quarter for a people with no herds, rising to the full amount for herders: {{ N['chain']['motive0'] }} + {{ 1-N['chain']['motive0'] }} × the share of their land they stock) with how organised its killing is (bands of foragers at {{ N['chain']['forager_org'] }} of settled or herding peoples). Each age class loses the effort × its vulnerability a year. Wyrmlings are always fully exposed (1): spears, traps and bait reach them. The young are exposed {{ N['chain']['vulnerability']['young'][0] }} + {{ N['chain']['vulnerability']['young'][1] }} × the tech level. Adults are exposed {{ N['chain']['vulnerability']['adult'][0] }} + {{ N['chain']['vulnerability']['adult'][1] }} × the tech level², so they are almost out of reach until the tech level is high. Ancients are exposed half as much as adults. The tech level comes from a calendar, not from what each people actually knows (the model works out its tech tree only after the history): stone age 0, bronze age {{ N['chain']['era_level']['bronze age'] }}, classical {{ N['chain']['era_level']['classical'] }}, medieval {{ N['chain']['era_level']['medieval'] }}, renaissance {{ N['chain']['era_level']['renaissance'] }}. The present is the tree's era (renaissance by default). The eras before it are counted back over Earth-like spans: renaissance {{ N['chain']['era_kyr']['renaissance'] }} kyr, medieval {{ N['chain']['era_kyr']['medieval'] }}, classical {{ N['chain']['era_kyr']['classical'] }} and bronze age {{ N['chain']['era_kyr']['bronze age'] }}, with the stone age before that. The kill lowers the young dragons' chance of reaching breeding age and the adults' years of breeding. On the dragons' own life table, births must rise by a factor F to make that up. The model lets births rise at most twofold, so the dragons settle at their unpressed density × 1 ÷ (1 + (F − 1)). That never reaches zero and never rises above the unpressed density. Which age class is hit matters a great deal. On the same life table, a kill of 0.2% a year falling on one class alone leaves {{ pct(N['literature']['kill_table']['one_stage_0p2pct']['wyrmling'],3) }} of the dragons if it falls on wyrmlings, {{ pct(N['literature']['kill_table']['one_stage_0p2pct']['young'],3) }} on the young, {{ pct(N['literature']['kill_table']['one_stage_0p2pct']['adult'],3) }} on adults and {{ pct(N['literature']['kill_table']['one_stage_0p2pct']['ancient'],3) }} on ancients. The wyrmling stage is short and many wyrmlings die anyway, so losing a few more costs little. The young stage is long, so a small kill there adds up over many years. The same pattern holds in other long-lived, slow-breeding animals: in loggerhead turtles, protecting eggs alone "could not prevent the eventual extinction of the model population" (Crouse et al. 1987, p. 1417). Giants have no life table in the model; they take the adults' vulnerability in a single-stage form, 1 ÷ (1 + kill/r), with r = ln 2 ÷ their generation. Overkill and hunting are separate channels: one is the herds' past loss of species, the other a present-day take. They compound, because the hunters eat first (Section 4).
