7. A Sword Coast-sized region

The Sword Coast, the coastal region where the best-known D&D setting's adventures most often take place, is roughly 1,500 miles north to south and 1,000 miles wide on most official maps: {{ f0(N['sword_coast']['ns_km']) }} km × {{ f0(N['sword_coast']['ew_km']) }} km = {{ sig(N['sword_coast']['km2']/1e6,3) }} million km². The counts below treat all of it as land.

A real region of the same size and shape. The Sword Coast is a long coast with the sea to the west, as is western Europe. A box of the same size centred on 47°N, 0.5°E runs from about {{ f1(N['sword_coast']['box']['lat_s']) }}°N to {{ f1(N['sword_coast']['box']['lat_n']) }}°N and {{ f1(-N['sword_coast']['box']['lon_w']) }}°W to {{ f1(N['sword_coast']['box']['lon_e']) }}°E, {{ sig(N['sword_coast']['box']['km2']/1e6,3) }} million km² on a sphere: from the Strait of Gibraltar to about the northern tip of Denmark, and from the Atlantic coasts of Ireland and Portugal to about the longitude of Munich. Much of it is sea; we have not measured how much, so the comparison is of size and shape only.

Is that a viable population? Marshall et al. estimate about 20,000 post-juvenile T. rex alive at any one time over 2.3 ± 0.88 million km², with a 95% interval of 1,300 to 328,000 individuals, and they reject the low end on "the loose consensus that at least thousands of individuals are needed for long-term persistence" (p. 285). Burness et al. give minimum viable populations as "frequent estimates are a few thousand to at least 10,000 individuals" (p. 14521). A Sword Coast-sized region under the model's present, mostly peopled, holds tens to hundreds of dragons of every kind together, counting wyrmlings; one kind holds a fraction of that. On those counts a region's dragons persist only as part of a much larger linked population. The model does treat pockets of one kind within {{ f0(5*1500) }} km of one another (five flights of 1,500 km) as one population, and lowers the viable number for long-lived kinds, but whether its test is strict enough at these counts has not been checked (Section 9, item 7). Burness et al.'s land-area lines (Section 5.4) make the same point from the other side: a land of this size has never held a top predator anywhere near a dragon's size.
