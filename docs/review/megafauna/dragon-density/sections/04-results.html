5.3 Where nobody lives: poor land and patchy habitat, not pressure

One might expect the peoples' pressure to explain the shortfall of dragons on rich empty land, biting a little even below the 0.02 people per km² threshold. It does not. Over all {{ len(N['runs']['on']) }} runs, the mean pressure in nobody's country now is {{ sig(cells('on','0','none_now','press_mean'),2) }} (at most {{ sig(cells('on','0','none_now','press_max'),2) }} in any cell), and on its rich land it is {{ rp('on','none','rich','press_mean') }}. The shortfall has two parts. First, most empty land is poor: {{ rp('on','none','poor','km2',2,1e-6/len(N['runs']['on'])) }} of its {{ sig(sum(N['cells']['on']['0']['rich_poor']['none'][b]['km2'] for b in ('poor','middling','rich'))*1e-6/len(N['runs']['on']),2) }} million km² per run carries under 1 t/km² of prey (mean {{ rp('on','none','poor','prey_t_km2') }} t/km², median latitude {{ rp('on','none','poor','lat_abs_median') }}°), where the food feeds only {{ rp('on','none','poor','food_fed_per_1000km2') }} dragons per 1,000 km². Second, occupancy: only {{ pct(cells('on','0','none_now','occupied_share'),2) }} of nobody's land holds any dragon kind, and {{ pct(cells('on','0','none_now','food_in_unoccupied_share'),2) }} of the dragons' food there lies in cells no kind lives in. 5.4 Against the literature

  • Carbone & Gittleman (2002): carnivores per 10,000 kg of prey = 89.1 M−1.05 (M in kg; N = 25, R² = 0.83; p. 2274). For a 1.65 t carnivore that is 1 per {{ sig(N['literature']['carbone_one_per_km2_at_1t'],3) }} km² at 1 t/km², {{ sig(N['literature']['carbone_one_per_km2_at_1t']/N['chain']['one_per_km2_at_1t'],2) }} times sparser than the model. Their abstract rounds the fit to 10,000 kg of prey supporting "about 90 kilograms of a given species of carnivore" (p. 2273); that is the fit's value at 1 kg. At 1.65 t the same fit gives {{ sig(N['literature']['carbone_biomass_kg_per_10t'],2) }} kg of carnivore per 10,000 kg of prey; the model's dragons come to {{ sig(N['literature']['model_biomass_kg_per_10t_eco'],3) }} kg at the ecological mass, {{ sig(N['literature']['model_biomass_kg_per_10t_eco']/N['literature']['carbone_biomass_kg_per_10t'],2) }} times as much. Two caveats: the fit's heaviest species is the polar bear at 310 kg, so 1.65 t is five times past its data; and its prey is the main prey species, those making up 70% or more of the diet (note 21, p. 2275), not all herbivores. Carbone et al. (2011) expect that "ectothermic theropods" would "obtain higher densities for a given density of prey" (p. 2687), which is the direction the model goes.
  • Jetz et al. (2004): area per individual for mammalian carnivores, 34.43 ha × M0.86 (n = 38; Table 1, p. 268), gives 1 per {{ sig(N['literature']['jetz_carn_km2_each'],3) }} km² at 1.65 t whatever the prey; the model's line crosses it at {{ sig(N['literature']['jetz_crossing_t_km2'],2) }} t/km². The same table's carnivore home range, 52.07 ha × M1.20 (n = 70), gives {{ sig(N['literature']['jetz_carn_home_range_km2'],2) }} km² at 1.65 t, a disc of {{ sig((N['literature']['jetz_carn_home_range_km2']/math.pi)**0.5,2) }} km radius. The model's 60 km foraging reach, a disc of about 11,300 km², is three times that. Home ranges overlap, which is why Jetz et al.'s area per animal is far smaller than the home range (p. 268).
  • Farlow (1993), reptiles: density = 66.07 M−1.01 per km² (M in kg), Damuth's reptile data with Komodo dragon data added (p. 187), gives 1 per {{ sig(N['literature']['farlow_reptile_one_per_km2'],2) }} km² at 1.65 t. Farlow notes that the largest reptile in the data is more than two orders of magnitude lighter than the predators he applies it to (p. 187). The model reaches this density only on {{ sig(N['literature']['farlow_reptile_crossing_t_km2'],2) }} t/km² of prey.
  • Marshall et al. (2021) estimate T. rex abundance from Damuth's law, density = a M−0.75 per km² with M in g, giving T. rex a physiology "midway between that of mammalian carnivores and that of large varanid lizards" (p. 284): log10 a = 2.99, with a 95% interval of 1.80 to 4.18 (Table 1, p. 284). At the dragons' ecological mass that is 1 per {{ sig(N['literature']['marshall_one_per_km2']['mid'],2) }} km², with an interval of 1 per {{ sig(N['literature']['marshall_one_per_km2']['lo'],2) }} to 1 per {{ sig(N['literature']['marshall_one_per_km2']['hi'],2) }} km² (our arithmetic). The model matches the central value only on {{ sig(N['literature']['marshall_prey_match_t_km2'],2) }} t/km² of prey; on the land most dragons live on, it is sparser than Marshall's line but well inside its interval, which spans a factor of {{ sig(N['literature']['marshall_one_per_km2']['lo']/N['literature']['marshall_one_per_km2']['hi'],2) }}. Marshall's line reads no prey.
  • Farlow (1993), the prey to predator ratio. In modern large-mammal communities the prey to predator biomass ratio is "usually somewhere between 200:1 and 100:1" but "can get as low as 45:1" (p. 176). His 50:1 and 10:1 are not observations but best-case minimum density ratios, with the predators eating all the prey's production (pp. 171–172): "at least 50:1" for an endotherm as large as its prey and "at least 10:1" for an ectotherm (p. 187), a case he calls "a limiting, extreme case" (p. 187). The model's dragons alone hold a prey to dragon biomass ratio of {{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_mean'],3) }}:1, counting dragons of every age at their own mass ({{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_eco'],2) }}:1 at the ecological mass). It is tempting to read this as fitting an animal that eats at a reptile's rate. That reads Farlow backwards. Real mammal guilds sit two to four times above Farlow's endotherm floor, so on his reasoning a guild eating at a reptile's rate would sit well below the mammals' band, not inside it. And where the fossil ratio is high, he draws the opposite conclusion: "if we want to argue that tyrannosaurs were more reptile-like than mammal-like or bird-like in their food requirements, we must then conclude that tyrannosaur populations were regulated by some factor(s) that kept them considerably smaller than their food requirements alone would have dictated" (p. 190). His counts of real big-theropod faunas run from about 10:1 (articulated skeletons, Judith River) to a mean of 115:1 (Brinkman's screen-washed teeth) (p. 189), and he declines to choose: the ratio "could have been as low as 10:1 or as high as 100:1" (p. 190). The model's {{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_eco'],2) }}:1 to {{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_mean'],3) }}:1 sits at the top of that range. Most of the gap to Farlow's ectotherm floor is the guild split, not the metabolism: the dragons get only {{ pct(N['chain']['dragon_share'],2) }} of a 10% offtake. Adding the smaller predators at Carbone et al.'s class biomasses (the classes under 1 t hold {{ sig(N['literature']['guild_biomass_small_over_apex'],2) }} times the biomass of the classes of 1 t and up), the whole guild comes to about {{ sig(N['literature']['whole_guild_ratio_mean'],2) }}:1 counting dragons of every age and {{ sig(N['literature']['whole_guild_ratio_eco'],2) }}:1 at the ecological mass, at or below Farlow's lowest observed mammal case (our arithmetic).
  • Burness, Diamond & Flannery (2001) fit the largest species' mass against land area for each land mass (p. 14520). For a Sword Coast-sized land of 3.88 million km² their lines give a top carnivore of about {{ sig(N['literature']['burness_sc']['endo_carn'],2) }} kg if endothermic and {{ sig(N['literature']['burness_sc']['ecto_carn'],2) }} kg if ectothermic; dinosaurs ran about 12 times heavier than mammals for the same area (p. 14523), about {{ sig(N['literature']['burness_sc']['dino_offset_x12']/1000,2) }} t (our arithmetic). A region that size holding thousands of 1.65 t to 52 t predators is outside what any real land of its size has supported. Dragons that fly between regions are a partial answer: the population that matters is the planet's, not the region's.

In nobody's country the occupied points (squares) follow the model's line up to the richest land. The all-cells points (circles) fall below it as occupancy falls (Section 5.3). In peopled country the food-fed points sit near the line, and the dragons themselves sit at 0.01 to 0.1 per 1,000 km² whatever the prey: the persecution floor.
