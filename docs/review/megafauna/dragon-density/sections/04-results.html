5.4 Against the literature

  • Carbone & Gittleman (2002): carnivores per 10,000 kg of prey = 89.1 M−1.05 (M in kg; N = 25, R² = 0.83; p. 2274). For a 1.65 t carnivore that is 1 per {{ sig(N['literature']['carbone_one_per_km2_at_1t'],3) }} km² at 1 t/km², {{ sig(N['literature']['carbone_one_per_km2_at_1t']/N['chain']['one_per_km2_at_1t'],2) }} times sparser than the model. But the exponent "does not differ significantly from −1.0" (confidence limits −0.845 to −1.25; p. 2274), and the abstract states the rule at −1: 10,000 kg of prey supports "about 90 kilograms of a given species of carnivore, irrespective of body mass" (p. 2273). At exactly −1 the line gives a 1.65 t carnivore 1 per {{ sig(1/(89.1*1650**-1.0/10),3) }} km² at 1 t/km², and the model is {{ sig(1/(89.1*1650**-1.0/10)/N['chain']['one_per_km2_at_1t'],2) }} times denser: close. On the fitted −1.05 the same fit gives {{ sig(N['literature']['carbone_biomass_kg_per_10t'],2) }} kg of carnivore per 10,000 kg of prey; the model's dragons come to {{ sig(N['literature']['model_biomass_kg_per_10t_eco'],3) }} kg at the ecological mass, {{ sig(N['literature']['model_biomass_kg_per_10t_eco']/N['literature']['carbone_biomass_kg_per_10t'],2) }} times as much; that larger gap rests on the 0.05 that is not significantly different from zero. Two caveats: the fit's heaviest species is the polar bear at 310 kg, so 1.65 t is five times past its data; and its prey is the main prey species, those making up 70% or more of the diet (note 21, p. 2275), not all herbivores. Carbone et al. (2011) suggest one "might expect" that "ectothermic theropods" would "obtain higher densities for a given density of prey" (p. 2687), which is the direction the model goes.

Marshall's line reads no prey.
  • Farlow (1993), the prey to predator ratio. In modern large-mammal communities the prey to predator biomass ratio is "usually somewhere between 200:1 and 100:1" but "can get as low as 45:1" (p. 176). His 50:1 and 10:1 are not observations but best-case minimum density ratios, with the predators eating all the prey's production (pp. 172, 174): "at least 50:1" for an endotherm as large as its prey and "at least 10:1" for an ectotherm (p. 187), a case he calls "a limiting, extreme case" (p. 187). The model's dragons alone hold a prey to dragon biomass ratio of {{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_mean'],3) }}:1, counting dragons of every age at their own mass ({{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_eco'],2) }}:1 at the ecological mass). It is tempting to read this as fitting an animal that eats at a reptile's rate. That reads Farlow backwards. Real mammal guilds sit two to four times above Farlow's endotherm floor, so on his reasoning a guild eating at a reptile's rate would sit well below the mammals' band, not inside it. And where the fossil ratio is high, he draws the opposite conclusion: "if we want to argue that tyrannosaurs were more reptile-like than mammal-like or bird-like in their food requirements, we must then conclude that tyrannosaur populations were regulated by some factor(s) that kept them considerably smaller than their food requirements alone would have dictated" (p. 190). His counts of real big-theropod faunas, ratios of individuals or bones of plant-eating dinosaurs to tyrannosaurs rather than of biomass, run from about 10:1 (articulated skeletons, Judith River) to a mean of 115:1 (Brinkman's screen-washed teeth) (p. 189), and he declines to choose: the ratio "could have been as low as 10:1 or as high as 100:1" (p. 190). Farlow treats count and biomass ratios as comparable only "when the prey and predators are about the same individual mass" (p. 176), so the comparison that follows is loose. The model's {{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_eco'],2) }}:1 to {{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_mean'],3) }}:1 sits at the top of that range. Most of the gap to Farlow's ectotherm floor is the guild split, not the metabolism: the dragons get only {{ pct(N['chain']['dragon_share'],2) }} of a 10% offtake. Adding the smaller predators at Carbone et al.'s class biomasses (the classes under 1 t hold {{ sig(N['literature']['guild_biomass_small_over_apex'],2) }} times the biomass of the classes of 1 t and up), the whole guild comes to about {{ sig(N['literature']['whole_guild_ratio_mean'],2) }}:1 counting dragons of every age and {{ sig(N['literature']['whole_guild_ratio_eco'],2) }}:1 at the ecological mass, about at Farlow's lowest observed mammal case (our arithmetic).
  • Burness, Diamond & Flannery (2001) fit the mass of each land mass's top species (the one that eats the most) against the land's area (pp. 14518, 14520). For a Sword Coast-sized land of 3.88 million km² their lines give a top carnivore of about {{ sig(N['literature']['burness_sc']['endo_carn'],2) }} kg if endothermic and {{ sig(N['literature']['burness_sc']['ecto_carn'],2) }} kg if ectothermic; the top carnivorous dinosaurs ran about 12 times heavier than their lines for living ectotherms predict for the same area (p. 14523), about {{ sig(N['literature']['burness_sc']['dino_offset_x12']/1000,2) }} t (our arithmetic). A region that size holding thousands of 1.65 t to 52 t predators is outside what any real land of its size has supported. Dragons that fly between regions are a partial answer: the population that matters is the planet's, not the region's.

In nobody's country the occupied points (squares) follow the model's line up to the richest land. The all-cells points (dots) fall below it as occupancy falls (Section 5.3). In peopled country the food-fed points sit within a factor of two below the line, on Carbone & Gittleman's line between 0.4 and 4 t/km², and the dragons themselves sit well below it: the peoples take most of the food first, and now also kill them (Table 4).
