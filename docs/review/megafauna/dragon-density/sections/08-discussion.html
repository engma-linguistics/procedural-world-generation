8. Discussion

The form is standard; the content of each step is weak. The chain is the usual shared food budget. Farlow builds predator density the same way: predator density × mass × mass-specific intake = prey density × mass × turnover (Farlow 1993, eqs. 3 to 5, p. 172). The weaknesses are in what goes into each step: a herbivore fit fed the wrong kind of plant production, a flat offtake, one Cretaceous guild split for every world, a reptile intake law stretched 60 to 1,100 times past its data and then labelled as a reptile's, an unsourced assimilation, whole carcasses eaten, and a persecution rule whose form has support but whose size is a judgement. Section 3 shows that these choices partly cancel: a reading that follows the papers at each step gives {{ X(N['sensitivity']['combined'][1]['factor']) }} of the headline, but the band around it is roughly ten either way.

Where nobody lives, the model's formula is defensible, but not for the obvious reason. The formula, 1 dragon of 1.65 t per {{ sig(N['chain']['one_per_km2_at_1t'],3) }} km² per t/km² of prey, sits between the mammal-carnivore lines (Carbone & Gittleman; Jetz et al.) and the reptile line (Farlow) at the prey levels where most dragons live, and inside Marshall et al.'s interval for T. rex. The realised density is lower: nobody's country holds {{ sig(era('on','0','none'),2) }} per 1,000 km², just under the bottom of Marshall et al.'s interval ({{ sig(1000*N['literature']['marshall_per_km2']['lo'],2) }} per 1,000 km²), because most empty land is poor and much of it unheld (Section 5.3). But it is tempting to add that the dragons' prey to dragon ratio of about {{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_mean'],2) }}:1 "fits an animal that eats at a reptile's rate". Farlow's reasoning runs the other way (Section 5.4). If dragons eat like reptiles and still sit at a mammal guild's ratio, something other than food must hold them down; if they eat like mammals or birds, as fliers of their size plausibly must, the food alone would hold them at {{ X(S('Mammal')['factor']) }} of the model's density. Seymour's measurements on estuarine crocodiles bear on this: a 200 kg crocodile produces "only 14% of that for a mammal" in burst power, and crocodiles "lack not only the absolute power for exercise, but also the endurance" of mammals (Seymour 2013, abstract, p. 1). Seymour measures burst power, not daily intake, and no paper consulted gives a daily cost of flight; but on that evidence a dragon that flies is probably not a crocodile-like ectotherm, and its intake probably lies at or above the dinosaur estimates (22% to 31% of a mammal's). Which line to use is a design decision for the world's authors (Section 9, item 3), but the label "a reptile's rate" should go either way.

Where nobody lives, what limits dragons now is poor land and habitat. The pressure is not the cause of the shortfall on empty land: over {{ len(N['runs']['on']) }} runs it is near zero there. Most empty land is poor and cold, and the model's dragon kinds live on only {{ pct(cells('on','0','none_now','occupied_share'),2) }} of it; {{ pct(cells('on','0','none_now','food_in_unoccupied_share'),2) }} of the dragons' food there goes uneaten, above all in wild forest, tundra and grassland. That may be deliberate (the D&D roster's evil, colour-coded dragon kinds each have their own terrain), but it means the model's "1 per {{ sig(N['chain']['one_per_km2_at_1t'],3) }} km² per t/km²" applies to a minority of the land. If the world's authors want wild forest and grassland to hold dragons, the habitat model, not the food chain, is what must change.

Where peoples live, early losses come through the food, late ones through the kill. Persecution and overkill together leave {{ pct(min(P('0',c,'median') for c in ('others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} to {{ pct(max(P('0',c,'median') for c in ('others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} of the pressure-off density in peopled country now (medians, Table 3). The pressure-off arm still has the hunters eating, so with their take counted too the dragons keep less: by the rough product of Table 4, {{ chall('0','farmers') }} in farmers' country and {{ chall('0','foragers') }} in foragers'. Persecution now follows the owner's ruling: very slight with stone-age means, rising with tech, never fatal. The kill falls by age class, so early peoples reach the wyrmlings, hardly the young and not the adults, and the dragons' life table makes a wyrmling kill cheap. In the stone age persecution leaves {{ chk('25','foragers','persecution') }} of the dragons in foragers' country and {{ chk('25','farmers','persecution') }} in farmers' (Table 4); with renaissance tech it leaves {{ chk('0','farmers','persecution') }} in farmers' country. The form of this rule has support. Large carnivores' slow reproduction leaves them "poorly able to respond to persecution" (Ripple et al. 2014, p. 1241484-1). Among carnivores, slow life history predicts extinction risk where people are dense, at 10 or more per km² (Cardillo et al. 2004, Tables 2–3, pp. 0911–0912). In long-lived animals, growth rests on the long juvenile and adult stages, not on the first year (Crouse et al. 1987, p. 1417). Its size does not have support. KILL_Q is set so that a cell of dense renaissance farmers (10 per km²) keeps 10% of its dragons to persecution alone, and that is a judgement. (Farmed land is thinner: its median cell holds {{ sig(N['cells']['on']['0']['biome_situation']['table']['forest']['farmed']['people_km2_median'],2) }} people per km² in forest and {{ sig(N['cells']['on']['0']['biome_situation']['table']['grassland']['farmed']['people_km2_median'],2) }} in grassland, against {{ sig(N['cells']['on']['0']['biome_situation']['table']['grassland']['city hinterland']['people_km2_median'],2) }} in a city's grassland hinterland. Persecution in farmers' country as a whole leaves {{ chk('0','farmers','persecution') }}.) The evidence read does not pin it either way. Ripple et al.'s tiger and lion hold 18% and 17% of their historical ranges (Table 1). That is a share of range, not of density, but it shows that large carnivores are pushed out of most farmed land, so a harsher setting with local extirpation would be defensible. Their large carnivores also still hold on average 47% of their ranges (p. 1241484-1), and some species tolerate much more human land use than others (p. 1241484-6), so a milder setting is defensible too. "Never fatal" does not constrain the choice: the rule's form never reaches zero at any setting (Section 2, step 7). The owner can now move it in the viewer. The tech level comes from a calendar of eras, a proxy for what each people actually knows (Section 9).

What persecution no longer explains is the early loss. At 25 kyr ago foragers' country keeps {{ pct(P('25','foragers','median'),2) }} of the pressure-off density, and nearly all of that loss is overkill of the wild herds ({{ chk('25','foragers','overkill') }} left). On top of it the hunters' own take, which is in every arm, leaves only {{ chk('25','foragers','offtake') }} of the herds' surplus for the dragons. Three choices make these food channels this strong. First, the setting's hunting peoples are dense: halflings at 12 people per km², goblins at 5. Real foragers live at about 0.03 to 0.13 people per km². Hamilton et al. fit the area a forager group uses, ln(A/km²), against the log of its size (all foragers: exponent 0.70, constant 4.44 in Table 1, p. 4767; the same fit is labelled 0.70x + 4.14 on Fig. 1, p. 4766). Groups of 25 to 1,000 then live at {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton']['4.44']['25'],1) }} to {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton']['4.44']['1000'],1) }} people per km² with the table's constant, or {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton']['4.14']['25'],1) }} to {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton']['4.14']['1000'],2) }} with the figure's, around a scatter of about two orders of magnitude (r² = 0.24). Their 2009 fit, of area against a group's total body mass (Fig. 2, p. 12257), gives {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton2009']['25']['55'],1) }} to {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton2009']['1000']['45'],2) }} people per km² for the same groups, taking 45 to 55 kg a person (our assumption). Zhu et al. find that a meat-heavy diet goes with low density ("high fractions of meat in the diet, leading to greatly reduced population density due to trophic inefficiency", Zhu et al. 2021, abstract). So dense peoples who eat half their food as big game are what the data rule out. Second, every tonne the hunters take comes out of the dragons' cattle-sized prey. Third, the flat 60% overkill ignores Sandom et al.'s main finding: losses depend on how long people and fauna lived together, from about 2% of species where they co-evolved to about 46% where modern humans arrived first (Fig. 3, p. 5). On this world the peoples evolved beside the herds. The options are left to the owner (inbox draft "forager offtake overstated"); this round changes none of them.

A region's single kind falls short of a viable population. On the model's present a Sword Coast-sized region holds {{ sig(min(sc('0',c,'on') for c in ('none','others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} to {{ sig(max(sc('0',c,'on') for c in ('none','others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} dragons of all kinds and ages (Table 8), a single kind a fraction of that, where the literature puts a viable population at thousands (Marshall et al. 2021, p. 285; Burness et al. 2001, p. 14521). Flying dragons linked across continents can be one population; but the model's regional counts make dragons a planet-wide metapopulation, not a regional fauna, and the game present should be read that way.

The deep past is set by a rule, not a budget. With the pressure off, the food budget gives {{ X(N['steps']['off']['300_summary']['median']) }} the flat rule's dragons at the 300 kyr hand-over on the median, so the rule is about right on average; but run by run it misses by up to a factor of {{ sig(max(N['steps']['off']['300_summary']['max'], 1/N['steps']['off']['300_summary']['min']),2) }}, and it leaves the peoples out. The "age of dragons" is real in the model's story, but its size in any one world is a rule's, not its land's.
