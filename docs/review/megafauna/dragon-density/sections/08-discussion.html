8. Discussion

The form is standard; the content of each step is weak. The chain is the usual shared food budget. Farlow builds predator density the same way: predator density × mass × mass-specific intake = prey density × mass × turnover (Farlow 1993, eqs. 3 to 5, p. 172). The weaknesses are in what goes into each step: a herbivore fit fed the wrong kind of plant production, a flat offtake, one Cretaceous guild split for every world, a reptile intake law stretched 60 to 1,100 times past its data and then labelled as a reptile's, an unsourced assimilation, whole carcasses eaten, and a pressure rule with no source at all. Section 2 shows that these choices partly cancel: the headline lands within a few per cent of a reading that follows the papers at each step ({{ X(N['sensitivity']['combined'][1]['factor']) }}), but inside a band of roughly ten either way.

Where nobody lives, the model is defensible but not for the reason round 1 gave. A 1.65 t dragon at 1 per {{ sig(N['chain']['one_per_km2_at_1t'],3) }} km² per t/km² of prey sits between the mammal-carnivore lines (Carbone & Gittleman; Jetz et al.) and the reptile line (Farlow), and inside Marshall et al.'s interval for T. rex. But round 1 also argued that the dragons' prey to dragon ratio of about {{ sig(N['chain']['prey_dragon_biomass_ratio_mean'],2) }}:1 "fits an animal that eats at a reptile's rate". Farlow's reasoning runs the other way (Section 4.4). If dragons eat like reptiles and still sit at a mammal guild's ratio, something other than food must hold them down; if they eat like mammals or birds, as fliers of their size plausibly must, the food alone would hold them at {{ X(S('Mammal')['factor']) }} of the model's density. Seymour's measurements on estuarine crocodiles bear on this: a 200 kg crocodile produces "only 14% of that for a mammal" in burst power, and crocodiles "lack not only the absolute power for exercise, but also the endurance" of mammals (Seymour 2013, abstract, p. 1). A dragon that flies cannot be a crocodile-like ectotherm; its intake should lie at or above McNab's mesotherm line. Which line to use is a design decision for the owner (Section 7, item 3), but the label "a reptile's rate" should go either way.

Where nobody lives, what limits dragons now is habitat. Round 1 blamed the pressure for the shortfall on rich empty land; over 24 runs the pressure there is near zero. The model's dragon kinds simply do not live on most of the richest empty land, above all on wild grassland and forest. That may be deliberate (chromatic dragons have their own terrains), but it means the model's "1 per 159 km² per t/km²" applies to a minority of the land. If the owner wants rich wild land to hold dragons, the habitat model, not the food chain, is what must change.

Where peoples live, the pressure decides almost everything, and it has no source. Together the channels make peopled country {{ sig(1/max(P('0',c,'median') for c in ('others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} to {{ sig(1/min(P('0',c,'median') for c in ('others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} times emptier of dragons than with the pressure off (medians, Table 2). By far the largest is persecution (Table 3), a rule that presses every dragon toward 1 per 5,000 km² at any settled density, with both constants hand-set. Ripple et al. measure range, not density; their large carnivores still hold on average 47% of their historical ranges, and species differ widely in how much human land use they tolerate (pp. 1241484-1, -6). Hunting adds to it, above all in foragers' country, where the peoples' share is inflated by the dnd tree's dense "hunter" peoples (halflings at 12 people per km², goblins at 5), while real foragers live at about 0.03 to 0.13 people per km². Hamilton et al. fit the area a forager group uses, ln(A/km²), against the log of its size (all foragers: exponent 0.70, constant 4.44 in Table 1, p. 4767; the same fit is labelled 0.70x + 4.14 on Fig. 1, p. 4766); groups of 25 to 1,000 then live at {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton']['4.44']['25'],1) }} to {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton']['4.44']['1000'],1) }} people per km² with the table's constant, {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton']['4.14']['25'],1) }} to {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton']['4.14']['1000'],2) }} with the figure's, around a scatter of about two orders of magnitude (r² = 0.24). Their 2009 fit, of area against a group's total body mass (Fig. 2, p. 12257), gives, taking 45 to 55 kg a person (our assumption), {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton2009']['25']['55'],1) }} to {{ sig(N['literature']['hamilton2009']['1000']['45'],2) }} people per km² for the same groups. The overkill channel adds a further factor, and its flat 60% of wild biomass ignores Sandom et al.'s main finding: losses depend on how long people and fauna lived together, from about 2% of species where they co-evolved to about 46% where modern humans arrived first (Fig. 3, p. 5).

Regional counts fall short of a viable population. On the model's present a Sword Coast-sized region holds tens to hundreds of dragons of all kinds and ages (Table 7), where the literature puts a viable population at thousands (Marshall et al. 2021, p. 285; Burness et al. 2001, p. 14521). Flying dragons linked across continents can be one population; but the model's regional counts make dragons a planet-wide metapopulation, not a regional fauna, and the game present should be read that way.

The deep past is overstated. The flat rule before 300 kyr gives {{ sig(1/N['steps']['off']['300_summary']['median'],2) }} times the food budget's dragons at the hand-over even with the pressure off. The "age of dragons" is real in the model's story but its size is set by a rule, not a budget.
