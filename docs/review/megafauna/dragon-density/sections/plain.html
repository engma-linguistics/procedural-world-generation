The question: how many dragons can a world, a region and a biome hold, and how does that change as rival predators and peoples compete for the same food? As the code is set now (the defaults), a world holds about {{ sig(N['headline']['rows']['persecution']['realised']['world'],2) }} dragons of every kind and age today. A Sword Coast-sized region holds about {{ sig(N['headline']['rows']['persecution']['realised']['region'],2) }}. A 10,000 km² stretch holds about {{ sig(N['headline']['rows']['persecution']['realised']['none'],2) }} on empty land and {{ sig(N['headline']['rows']['persecution']['realised']['peopled'],2) }} on peopled land (peopled land is the richer land; with only the hunters' take it would hold {{ sig(N['headline']['rows']['hunt']['realised']['peopled'],2) }}). The food alone, with no rival predators and no peoples, would feed about {{ sig(N['headline']['rows']['alone']['capacity']['world'],2) }} on the same worlds. Most of that gap is the smaller predators that share the dragons' prey, and the dragon kinds living only in their own kinds of country. The peoples' hunting, their overkill of the herds and their killing of dragons each take a further part. Each kind of competition is now a setting in the viewer, so the owner can see what the world is set to and change it. The two tables just below give the numbers.

The model works out how many dragons the land can feed. Plants feed grazing herds, and the model lets a tenth of the herds be eaten each year. Peoples, giants and smaller predators take their part, and the dragons get what is left of their share. Where nobody else eats, that comes to about one dragon per {{ sig(N['chain']['one_per_km2_at_1t'],2) }} km² for every tonne of grazers per km². That figure is uncertain by roughly ten times either way, and how much a dragon eats is the largest single unknown. On grassland it may be lower still, because the herd formula is fed more plant growth than it was built for.

The figures from here on are averages over all {{ len(N['runs']['on']) }} runs, not the six worlds of the headline, so they differ a little from it. Where nobody lives, the model's worlds hold about {{ sig(era('on','0','none'),2) }} dragons per 1,000 km² today (one per {{ sig(1000/era('on','0','none'),2) }} km²), counting every kind and age. That is fewer than the food allows: most empty land is poor and cold, and dragon kinds live on only {{ pct(cells('on','0','none_now','occupied_share'),2) }} of it.

Where peoples live, they cut the dragons three ways. They kill dragons. They have hunted out many of the big wild herds the dragons eat. And their hunters take their share of what is left before the dragons do. This round changed the killing to follow the owner's rulings. Stone-age peoples can reach the young wyrmlings but hardly the grown dragons, so they kill very few. The killing grows with weapons, numbers and herds to defend, and it never wipes a kind out. Today the killing and the lost herds together leave {{ pct(min(P('0',c,'median') for c in ('others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} to {{ pct(max(P('0',c,'median') for c in ('others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} of the dragons the land would hold if the peoples only hunted (in round 2 it was 1% to 3%). Counting the hunters' share of the food as well, the dragons keep fewer still: roughly {{ chall('0','farmers') }} in farmers' country and {{ chall('0','herders') }} in herders'. How hard late peoples kill is a judgement: it is set so that a crowded farming district of the renaissance keeps a tenth of its dragons. The evidence would allow harsher or milder, and the owner can now change it in the viewer. Long ago it is the food, not the killing, that costs the dragons. 25,000 years ago, in hunters' country, the killing and the lost herds leave only {{ pct(P('25','foragers','median'),2) }}, almost all of it through the herds. The setting's hunting peoples are far denser than real hunter-gatherers, and they eat the dragons' own prey. Whether that is too harsh is a decision for the owner, with the options in an inbox draft.

For a game set in the late Middle Ages, the model gives about {{ sig(gpv('grassland','wild'),2) }} dragons per 10,000 km² (a square 100 km on a side) in wild grassland, {{ sig(gpv('desert','wild'),2) }} in wild desert and {{ sig(gpv('mountain','wild'),2) }} in wild mountains. In farmed country it gives {{ sig(min(v for b in ('forest','grassland','desert','tundra','mountain') for v in [gpv(b,'farmed')] if v is not None),2) }} to {{ sig(max(v for b in ('forest','grassland','desert','tundra','mountain') for v in [gpv(b,'farmed')] if v is not None),2) }} by biome{{ (lambda v: f', and {sig(v,2)} in farmed wetland, a small share of the land' if v is not None else '')(gpv('wetland','farmed')) }}. In the model, a region the size of the Sword Coast holds {{ sig(min(sc('0',c,'on') for c in ('others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} to {{ sig(max(sc('0',c,'on') for c in ('others','foragers','herders','farmers')),2) }} dragons of every kind together where peoples live, and about {{ sig(sc('0','none','on'),2) }} where nobody does. The literature puts a viable population at thousands. {{ 'Only some of these regions reach that' if min(sc('0',c,'on') for c in ('none','others','foragers','herders','farmers')) < 1000 <= max(sc('0',c,'on') for c in ('none','others','foragers','herders','farmers')) else ('None of these regions reaches that' if max(sc('0',c,'on') for c in ('none','others','foragers','herders','farmers')) < 1000 else 'Each of these regions reaches that') }}, and a single kind of dragon is a fraction of each count, so the dragons are best read as one population spread across the world.

The literature finds several parts of the model wrong or weak. The most important are these. The food channels in hunters' country rest on peoples far denser than real ones. The herd formula is fed the wrong kind of plant growth. The dragons' food intake is labelled as a reptile's when it behaves like something between a reptile and a mammal. Section 9 lists the changes, each with its evidence and how it would move the numbers.
