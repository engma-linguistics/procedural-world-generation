Proper cultures: a proposal (languages, names, polities, attitudes, gods)

A draft to iterate on, written 2026-11-01 from the repo, the tickets and your ethnonym taxonomy. Nothing here is built. The 19 decisions are in §5, each with a lean. Source of truth: docs/review/cultures-proposal/proposal.md.

For Daniel, 2026-11-01, to iterate on. Written read-only against main at 02a3b147 (cultures round 2 merged; lane 109, overlapping demes, still running). Nothing here is built. Sources are listed at the end with what was actually read.

In plain words

Today a "culture" is a regional branch of a people that has lived apart long enough, or taken to new country. It knows where it lives, when it split, whom it came from, two drifting skills (sea craft, open-country herding) and its way of life. That is a good skeleton. It has no language, no name of its own beyond a placeholder, no ruler, no gods and no opinion of its neighbours.

The proposal is to put flesh on it in thin layers, each one a cheap pass after the run (like the demes and cultures are now), so we can look at it in the viewer and argue about it before anything feeds back into the history.

  1. Stage 0, languages without words. Each culture speaks a lect. Lects form a dated family tree that follows the culture tree. Every pair of neighbouring lects gets a mutual-intelligibility number worked out from how long ago they split, how much they have been in touch since, and a published rate of vocabulary loss. A dialect continuum falls out on its own: neighbours along a chain understand each other, the ends don't. With the sourced rate, two lects out of touch cross the language/dialect line after about 1.3 kyr (Wichmann 2019 puts it at 1.1-1.6 kyr). "Languages" are then clusters of lects on the near side of that line, derived rather than stored. Light history comes for free: splits, koines when sisters merge, shifts when one culture is swallowed by another, deaths, and borrowing episodes (including which food words would have travelled where, with the word forms left empty). Placeholder names follow your ethnonym taxonomy as glosses: "the true people", "the speakers", and per neighbour "the mutes" or "the people of the first valley we met".
  2. Stage 1, polities. Chiefdoms, states and empires grow out of the settlement ledger where the tech allows and the land is hemmed in (Carneiro), reach as far as their tech lets them rule (half a day's walk for a chiefdom, 50-250 km for an early state, more with writing, roads, horses and ships), and fight on their borders. War between polities is where the leader brake you parked (the peoples' share) can finally live, but I'd be honest up front: the literature lane 101 read says war mostly costs land, not lives, so polities will trim a dominant people, not topple it. Polities also give languages their main lever: a state's language spreads and its subjects shift.
  3. Stage 2, attitudes, gods, names with history, writing. Attitudes between cultures (from temperament plus what happened between them), a placeholder pantheon per culture whose domains follow its way of life, name stacks that change with conquest and contact (your P1-P8), and writing as a tech that makes standards, longer memories and in-world scholars' names for vanished peoples.

All of it reaches the culinary brief: per region and era, who lives here, what they speak and how close it is to the neighbours', who rules them and since when, what they call themselves and what others call them, and which food words came from where. No actual words, sounds or grammar until the language demo (PW-986), which this builds the plumbing for.

My strongest opinions, flagged so you can push back: a culture is not a language (the count of lects will be about the count of cultures; the count of languages will be much lower); keep everything output-side until you have looked at it, then move only polities (and war) into the history; and don't expect polities to fix the commonest-people share on their own.

2. The proposal, staged

Stage 0: placeholder language infrastructure (no words, no sounds)

2.0.1 What a language object is

One lect per culture. Every culture speaks exactly one lect, and the lect is born, splits and ends with it. That keeps one tree, not two, and it is what PW-981 meant when it called demes "speech/breeding populations".

A "language" is derived, not stored. Group lects into languages by single-linkage on mutual intelligibility at or above a threshold (Decision L2). So "the language" of a dialect chain is the whole chain, and a culture can be "a dialect of X". This matches how linguists actually draw the line (badly, and with politics), and it lets a polity later promote a lect to a language without touching the lect tree (Stage 1: the standard).

Families are what in-world scholars could see. The true lect tree goes back as far as the demes (300 kyr). A "family" is a clade whose members still share enough to be recognised as related: in practice, everything under a node younger than the comparative horizon (~8 kyr, a constant to argue over, Decision L4). Deeper nodes are kept but flagged beyond_horizon: the truth is underneath, as with PW-906's folklore. Isolates fall out naturally: a lect whose nearest relative split 15 kyr ago is an isolate to its scholars.

The horizon need not be a constant. With the rate in 2.0.2 (s = 0.92 x 0.72^(2t)), inherited similarity falls to ~0.03 by ~5 kyr at the mean rate. Slow lineages (the lognormal tail) stay above that for longer. If "recognisably related" means s_inherited above a chance floor (a judgement, ~0.03-0.05), the horizon comes out per family at roughly 5-9 kyr. That is close to your 6-10 kyr without setting it, which is PW-982's own acceptance test, met early by the bookkeeping.

Families' placeholder scholarly names follow your "language reconstruction" row: either the root place ("Rilunic") or, Indo-European style, the two far ends of the family's range ("Tholosas-Drama"). Cheap and very much in character.

What it carries in Stage 0, all as numbers or glosses: - id, culture, people, parent lect, born (split date and cause: gap, sea crossing, distance, adapted country, founding), ended and how (died out, merged by koine, shifted to X); - the pairwise similarities live on the pairs, not the lect (see 2.0.2); - rate_x: its own rate multiplier (lognormal, drawn at birth; see 2.0.2); - speakers now and at each frame (from its culture's demes and the history's density); - an L2 share (adult second-language learners: from shift events, mixing in cities and, later, polities); - optionally the PW-985 morphology scalar (Decision L7); - written: whether its culture knows writing, and from when (Stage 2 uses it); - a glossonym (2.0.6).

2.0.2 Mutual intelligibility from time, contact and a rate

The quantity to simulate is an ASJP-style lexical-phonetic similarity s between two lects. Holman et al. (2011) define it as 1 - LDND. LDN is the Levenshtein distance between same-meaning words on a 40-item list, divided by the longer word's length. LDND normalises LDN by the distance between different-meaning words. s covers both word replacement and sound change in one number, and published dates, thresholds and intelligibility results are all expressed in it. That is why I would not model word loss and sound change as two separate scalars. One number with a calibration beats two numbers without one.

1. Divergence with no contact. Holman et al. 2011 fitted t = (ln s - ln s0) / (2 ln r) on 52 dated groups, with s0 = 0.92 and r = 0.72 per millennium (t in kyr). The correlation of ln s with t is -0.84, and the mean absolute error is 29%. We run it forwards: s(t) = s0 · r^(2t). Each lineage loses at λ = -ln r ≈ 0.33 per kyr, scaled by its own rate_x. The 29% scatter is the honest size for that multiplier: a lognormal with sigma ≈ 0.3. Rates differ strongly by word (Pagel et al. 2007, abstract: word frequency explains about half the variation in replacement rates), so a fixed clock would be the wrong picture.

2. Contact pulls the surface back. While two lects are in contact, borrowing and levelling raise their similarity at a rate κ times the contact intensity c_ab. c_ab is the deme pair's border edges and shared cells per frame, already in demes.json. Per frame step dt:

s' = s + [ -(λa + λb) · s + κ · c_ab · (s0 - s) ] · dt

With steady contact the pair settles at s* = s0 · κc / (λa + λb + κc). Neighbours stay close however old their split. Along a chain, similarity falls off link by link. That is the dialect continuum, with no extra rule. Nerbonne (2010) names the observed shape Séguy's curve: aggregate dialect distance grows with the logarithm of geographic distance. The fit is better with travel time than with straight-line distance, and geography explains only about a quarter of the variance (r 0.41-0.62 across seven datasets). That last point is a useful warning: a model whose map looks too tidy is wrong. The lognormal rate_x and the uneven contact give the scatter.

Two similarities per pair keep the scholars honest: - s_inherited: never pulled back. This is what the comparative method sees, and families use it. - s_surface: pulled back by contact. This is what a listener meets, and intelligibility uses it.

Loans between unrelated lects raise s_surface a little and never touch s_inherited.

3. Where is the language line. Wichmann (2019) proposes LDN = 0.51 as a universal language/dialect cut-off. That is LDND ≈ 0.60, so s ≈ 0.40. He puts it at 1,075-1,635 years of separation (centre ~1,355). Our formula with no contact gives 1.27 kyr for the same s, which is a consistency check, not a coincidence: same database. His table places real pairs on the scale:

pair LDN
Indonesian-Malay 0.12
Hindi-Urdu 0.43
Russian-Belarusian 0.46
Danish-Swedish 0.49
Cairo-Moroccan Arabic 0.58
Catalan-Spanish 0.66

This is what I'd use for L2's bands.

Set against the cultures: T_APART_KYR is 0.15-0.6 kyr. A culture split 0.6 kyr ago with no contact has s ≈ 0.62 (LDND ≈ 0.38), so it is still a dialect of its parent. Cultures split well before languages do. Expect many cultures per language, as on Earth.

4. Intelligibility from similarity. mi_inherent = f(s_surface), a logistic curve. No published closed-form fit exists. Gooskens and van Heuven (2020) give correlations and R² only, so the curve is ours, fitted to their points. What constrains it: - For listeners with minimal exposure ("inherent intelligibility"), lexical distance correlates with intelligibility at r = -0.95 in Germanic. Adding orthographic, syntactic, phonetic and affix distances, the stepwise R² over all families climbs from 0.57 to 0.77. - Within a near-cognate continuum (Scandinavian), phonetic distance is the better predictor: r ≈ -0.80 against a non-significant lexical r (Gooskens 2007). - Gooskens et al. 2017/18 (16 languages, 1,833 listeners, a spoken cloze test) found Danes and Swedes with no exposure at 44-56% correct. They suggest 40% as "a possible threshold" for receptive multilingualism to work.

So the curve passes through ~0.5 at Danish-Swedish (s ≈ 0.42), ~0.4 near Wichmann's line, and toward 1 as s approaches s0. Mark it a placeholder fitted to European close relatives.

5. Exposure and asymmetry. The big result in that literature: once real listeners are counted, exposure to the other language is "the most important variable, overriding all other variables". It correlates with intelligibility at r = 0.87-0.93 by family. Attitudes add almost nothing beyond it (Gooskens & van Heuven 2020). Most asymmetries are exposure. A residue is structural: Portuguese listeners understand Spanish better than the reverse even with minimal exposure, which the authors put down to Portuguese reduced syllables and its vowel inventory (Gooskens et al. 2017/18). So:

mi_ab = mi_inherent(s_surface) + (1 - mi_inherent) · E_ab

E_ab grows when: - b's speakers dominate the shared cells; - b's culture rules a's (Stage 1); - b is a written standard or the towns' trade speech.

E_ab is in 0..1, and it is a judgement. A small seeded structural asymmetry per pair stands in for the Portuguese case. Report mi_inherent (symmetric) and mi_ab, mi_ba (directional, the numbers the brief prints).

Checks, not targets (Earth as a loose anchor): - two lects ~1.3 kyr apart with no contact sit at the language line; - a walker people's range with open borders shows a chain whose far ends fall below the line while every neighbour pair stays above it; - lect similarity against travel distance looks like Séguy's curve, with a lot of scatter; - lect density follows growing season as Nettle (1998) found: ln LANGS = 0.58 ln AREA + 0.23 MGS - 5.32 over 43 tropical countries (r = 0.66; MGS is growing-season months). That is about x1.26 languages per extra month at equal area. Ours should show the trend, not the constant: dragons and dwarves are not obliged to be tropical farmers; - different peoples' lects never show inherited similarity above chance.

2.0.3 Which pairs get a number

"Neighbours" = pairs whose demes touch now (border edges or shared cells in the latest frame), plus each lect's nearest sister in the tree. Typically a few per lect, so a few hundred numbers a run, stored sparse. Any other pair can be worked out on demand (the API computes it from the tree and the stored contact series), so the viewer's "intelligibility from here" map (section 4) needs no full matrix.

Different peoples: their lects are unrelated (s_inherited = 0, by construction: each stem found speech on its own, PW-947). They still get a contact number (borrowing, bilingualism), and mi_ab can be non-zero only through exposure (learned bilingualism), never structure.

Dragons and giants: a cohesive kind's lects are pulled together by cohesion the same way the culture drift is (one Draconic per colour today because there is one culture per colour). Peoples born of a megafauna stem (kobolds, dragonborn) could start speaking the stem's lect (D&D canon has kobolds speak Draconic) or their own: Decision L5.

2.0.4 Light history events

Written into languages.json as dated events, and into the brief and the Cultures tab as sentences: - split (date, cause, place): "the speech of the inland Drama humans parted from the coast's ~17 kyr ago when they moved up the rivers"; - koine (two sister cultures merged side by side): the new lect is the older culture's, its distance to the merged one set to zero, the merged one's history kept; - shift (a culture's last deme fused into another culture's deme, or a people's incursion settled among another): the shifting lect ends, the receiving lect records a substrate (who, when, how big), and its L2 share rises; - death (the last speakers died out or shifted); - contact episode (start, end, intensity, between lects or between peoples): the basis of borrowing; - standard (Stage 2: a written lect with a state); - horizon (a family's deepest visible node): "beyond ~8 kyr its relations are lost".

Language shift in Stage 0 rides only on what the demes and contacts already record. Real shift under states needs polities (Stage 1).

2.0.5 Contact borrowing (including food words, forms empty)

Per lect, a list of loan strata: from which lect or people, when, how much (a share of the notional list, from the contact integral), and of what kind: - core (rare, from the contact term above); - cultural (techs taken up by contact: the tech tree's contact front already says who learned what from whom and when; each such uptake writes a loan stratum "the word for the plough came from the X"); - food: every crop or animal taken up by spread or trade (the history's crops and animals, PW-719/721) writes a loan with its route: giver lect, relay lects, receiver. That is PW-984's skeleton with empty forms, and it is exactly the part of historical linguistics the culinary book prints. When PW-986 lands, the relay already exists; it only needs sound changes applied per hop (your X4 note). - substrate (from shift events): place names and words for the land's plants and animals (the classic substrate domain), flagged so a later pass knows where to put them.

Sizes from the World Loanword Database (Haspelmath & Tadmor 2009; 41 languages, 1,460 meanings). The mean borrowed share is ~25% (median 24%). The range runs from Mandarin 2% to Selice Romani 63%, with English at 42%. By field, the borrowed scores are: - social and political relations: 0.64; - religion and belief: 0.49; - agriculture and vegetation: 0.45; - food and drink: 0.37; - kinship: 0.23; - the body: 0.17; - spatial relations: 0.15.

Inside food the spread is what a cookbook would hope for: - traded and processed goods borrow heavily: olive 0.93, sugar 0.79, wine 0.74, beer and cheese 0.68, pepper 0.66, oil 0.60; - basic states and acts stay native: raw 0.05, cooked 0.06, to milk 0.07, to be hungry 0.03.

So a stratum's size is ~25% overall x a field weight, and a traded food almost always brings its word, a cooking verb almost never does. That sets how big each kind of stratum is, not which words.

2.0.7 What Stage 0 does not do

No phonology, inventories, syllables, forms, grammar or typology (PW-982/983). No sound change. No real shift dynamics (that needs states). No language across species boundaries except loans and learned bilingualism.

Stage 1: polities

2.1.1 What a polity is

A polity is a ruled territory with a centre: a set of cells at each 100-year step, a capital (a settlement ledger site), a ruling culture (and so a ruling lect), a tier, a founding date and a fate. Tiers, gated by techs that already exist: - band / tribe: no polity object; the culture is the unit (most of the map for most of history). - chiefdom: needs chiefdoms (villages + feasting). A centre and the villages within its reach. - state: needs kingship (chiefdoms + cities) or law (writing + chiefdoms). Delegated rule: reach well beyond the centre's day. - empire: a state that rules more than one culture of substance, or more than one people. Not a tech gate, a description, so it emerges.

2.1.2 Formation, growth and reach

  • Where chiefdoms start. Each step, each culture with chiefdoms and towns has a hazard of founding a chiefdom at its largest town. The hazard is scaled by:
  • local density;
  • resource concentration (rich fisheries, a river oasis);
  • a contagion term (Carneiro 2012: one chiefdom "spurred the formation of others");
  • circumscription (Carneiro 1970, read through his own 2012 restatement). Circumscription speeds states up but is "not absolutely essential". Social circumscription is "less tight", so states arise there too, "but it will take longer".

The in-fighting term already computes a people's circumscription C (free good country 0, other peoples' land SOCIAL_Q, sea and unlivable land 1). Hemmed-in, dense farming country makes chiefdoms and then states first; open frontiers keep people splitting off instead. This reuses counter._circumscribed, which is pleasing: the same number that today makes a people fight itself becomes the reason it submits to a chief. - Reach. A polity holds the cells within its reach in travel time from its capital (the demes' and crossing.py's travel costs; the brief's missing trade_reach gets built on the way). Reach grows by tier and tech: - A chiefdom reaches about half a day's travel from its centre, "some 25–30 km by foot" (Spencer 2010). That is a third to a fifth of an L7 cell, so a chiefdom is a one-cell thing at our grid. Honest consequence: chiefdoms are a tier label on a town and its cell, not a shape on the map. - A state is defined by ruling beyond a day's round trip. Spencer's six primary states reached 50–250 km (400 km for the Indus) soon after their state institutions appear. - Writing, law, roads, horses (riding) and ships extend that reach further.

Spencer's numbers are measured. How far each tech extends the reach is a judgement, checked against the sizes below. - Competition. Where reaches overlap, the stronger polity (population within reach x a tech factor x cohesion of the ruling culture) takes the cell after a contest; the weaker shrinks. Conquest is absorption of a weaker neighbour's core: logged as an event with a direction (feeds resented, P1/P2 names, shift). - Frontiers breed empires (Turchin 2009's mirror empires): nomad raiding pushes farming communities to unite, and large agrarian states push nomads to "band together". Turchin et al. 2013 rebuilt the Old World's imperial map from this at 100 km cells and 100-year steps. They seeded military technology at the steppe edge and let it spread, and the fit to history was R² = 0.65. With random seeding it fell to 0.17, and dropping the elevation term cut it to 0.48. So the lever is a herder-farmer frontier that spreads war techs. We have the pieces: open-country herders, the tech contact front, and the aggressive war-tech bonus. In this world it is a lovely hook: the human-orc steppe frontier, the dwarf mountains' edge, dragon country. Their grid and step are ours, which is reassuring. - Fragmentation and collapse. A polity has a base hazard of ending that does not grow with age (Arbesman 2011 found empire lifetimes exponential, mean ~220 years, i.e. ~0.46 per century). On top of it, the hazard rises with its size beyond its reach, its cultural mix, and bad years (droughts, plague later). Tiers move one step at a time, up and down: Currie et al. 2010's best-supported model for 84 Austronesian societies is "small steps" both ways (the model where falls may skip levels ranked second). So a collapsing empire normally falls to states, and those to chiefdoms. A big multi-step collapse can be a rare path. Successors take the provinces. A fallen empire leaves a name (X6: "names freeze at the moment of contact and outlive the dynasty") and ruins. - Size checks (not caps): the largest polity's area by era against Taagepera 1997 (nothing over 0.01 Mm² much before 3000 BC; the largest under 1.1 Mm² before ~650 BC and never under 2.3 Mm² after; record 5.5 Mm² Achaemenid, 24 Mm² Mongol). The two jumps come with "government at a distance" (bureaucracy, relay stations), so the jump should come from our bureaucracy and roads techs, not from a date. Durations: Taagepera's median is 130 years at half or more of peak (78 polities; only 16 lasted 300+ years). Turchin 2009 has 60+ "mega-empires" of 1 Mm² or more between 3000 BCE and 1800 CE, over 90% of them in or next to the arid belt. Polity population ladder (Johnson & Earle 2000, via Grinin 2003): simple chiefdoms in the thousands, complex chiefdoms in the tens of thousands, states in the hundreds of thousands or more. The bands overlap.

2.1.3 War, the brake you parked

The history already has the pieces: war-torn land K(W), with a state-pacification proxy G (tech level x town share) and a hostile-frontier share F of peoples. Polities replace the proxies with real things: - G becomes the polity's interior: inside a state's core, low-level feud is damped (Gómez et al. 2016; Turchin's -alpha S). - F becomes polity borders: frontier cells between hostile polities (of any peoples, including two human states) are war-torn. - In-fighting becomes polity fission (PW-1258): today every people "fights itself" where it is walled in. With polities, that fight is between and inside polities of one people: a crowded human landscape is many human states at war, which is what costs a leading people land. FEUD_CUT and SOCIAL_Q stay as they are for stateless country. - Sacking and razing (PW-1108): a site on a front of a conquest has a chance to be sacked or razed; walls (fortifications) halve it.

Honesty about the leader brake: lane 101 measured war deaths at the sourced violent-death rates at 0-0.2% of any people's numbers; the life tables already contain their peoples' violence. Polities will cost the commonest people some land along many internal borders, and states will spread the leader's language to others (which matters for the brief more than the share). If after this the humans are still ~60% on one world, I'd stop and take your D31 A as reported, not chase it with polity knobs. Plague (PW-1167) is the sourced brake on a dense farming leader.

This is the one place that wants to be in the history (Decision P1): war changes K, and K changes who holds land. I'd build polities v0 output-side first (read the ledger, techs, cultures; draw them; you look), then move the minimal state variable into the history once you like the shapes.

2.1.4 What polities give the other layers

  • Languages: a state's ruling lect has prestige. Shift in its territory follows Abrams & Strogatz 2003: dx/dt = y·c·x^a·s - x·c·y^a·(1 - s), x the share speaking the ruling lect, s its status. Their fitted exponent is a = 1.31 ± 0.25 over 42 regions (Peru, Scotland, Wales, Ireland, Bolivia, Alsace-Lorraine). The status values in their figure are 0.26-0.46. Their model has no stable coexistence. The authors note that status feedback (policy) can stabilise a bilingual state. So s comes from tier, writing and cities, and the shift is computed per cell per 100-year step. Currie & Mace 2009 (PNAS; 4,233 languages, 602 societies) give the large-scale check: political complexity is the largest predictor of a language's area (about a quarter of the explained variance, full-model R² 0.55). Levels 0 and 1 did not differ, and pastoralists' languages cover more ground than farmers'. Their mechanism is that complex societies "replace or incorporate less complex groups". The shift also gives an adult-L2 share, which feeds the morphology scalar (PW-985: empire languages simplify). A written state lect becomes a standard whose dialects converge on it (exposure). Cross-species shift becomes possible: humans in a dwarf state learn and, over generations, may speak dwarvish (Decision L5).
  • Names: E7 (position against a larger power), E11 (confederation), P1 (conquerors' name absorbed), P2 (newcomers inherit the old name), P4 (a constructed name at a founding), P5 (rejection at independence), X6 (dynasty or state).
  • Reputation: the resented valence (ruled by S), PW-756 closed properly.
  • Cultures: the merge-under-a-state rule in cultures.py (REACH_MKM2) becomes "merge within one polity", which is what PW-676 asked for.
  • Settlements: capitals, provincial centres, frontier forts, sackings.
  • Your Seven Meals empires (PW-785): either they emerge (the antelope-kin as a farming, milking people on a frontier is exactly a Turchin setting) or an authored [[event]] kind = "polity" anchors one as a back-fill.

Stage 2: attitudes, gods, names with history, writing

2.2.1 Attitudes (PW-904, PW-768)

Per ordered pair of cultures (observer, subject), a small vector instead of one aggressive trait: - xenophobia: temperament (the spec's per-people starting stance: orcs aggressive, elves insular) x difference (species distance: tier; culture distance: way of life, lect MI) x unfamiliarity (thin contact); - expansionism: temperament x crowding x polity frontier (Stage 1); - assimilation vs tolerance: does a polity of the observer's culture absorb or leave alone (feeds the shift rate); - grudge: conquest memory (reputation.py's strength), fading faster with intermarriage (mixing zones), shared faith (Stage 2 gods) and shared language (MI), slower with a stubbornness axis (per culture, inherited with drift).

Uses: the exonym valence (X5), the polity war hazard, the shift rate, city mixing (PW-910). Output-side v0, like reputation.py, which it grows out of (I'd extend reputation.py rather than start a new module).

2.2.2 Gods (PW-907, a placeholder)

Per culture a short pantheon: 3-7 god slots, each with domains and no names. Domains are drawn from what the culture lives by and fears: sea craft -> a sea god; herding -> a sky or storm god and a herd keeper; farming -> grain and fertility, a harvest feast; a volcano or quake belt nearby -> fire; dragon country -> a dragon cult or a dragon-slayer; the dead (burial techs). A daughter culture inherits the pantheon and drifts (a domain fades when the life it rested on fades, like the sea craft). Merging cultures syncretise (domains matched, one god absorbs the other, interpretatio); a polity can impose a state cult. Feeds: E10 endonyms, shared-faith grudge fading, and the brief's feasts and taboos hooks ("a harvest feast to the grain mother" as a suggested line). That's all I would build for v2.

2.2.3 Name stacks with history

Stage 0's stacks get their P-processes from Stage 1's events: conquest -> P1 or P2; independence -> P4 or P5; centuries of contact -> P6 (an ethnonym becoming a word: "a frank", "a vandal": only where a valence is strong); related lects -> P7 (one root split by borrowing). Exonyms relay along the contact graph (X4): a name that reached C through B records the relay path, ready for sound changes per hop later. PW-906's folklore forgetting fits here: the stack keeps the truth; the "what they believe" layer says "always been here" after ~300 years.

2.2.4 Writing as a tech

Writing already exists in the tech tree (tokens 10 kyr, proto-writing 8.6, writing floor 5.3, alphabet 3.8, law, bureaucracy, census). Proposed effects, all output-side: - a written lect of a state becomes a standard: dialects within the polity are pulled toward it (exposure), and its name stack gains a formal form (P8 parallel names); - memory of conquests lasts longer (reputation.py's ERA_FADE already does this by era; make it read writing instead); - scholarly names exist where writing did: a dead culture known to literate neighbours gets a "text" scholarly name (your Hittite and Sea Peoples rows); one known only from ruins gets a type-site or artifact name; - a script lineage per written lect: which script tradition it uses (inherited from whoever taught it writing, via the tech contact front; adopted with a state cult or a conquest). Placeholder ids only: this is the hook to your writing-systems repo, and the materials (clay, bark, hide, stone) can be read from the land and techs if you want that link later. - a literacy share per culture (tiny before bureaucracy and schools), for the brief and the morphology scalar (written standards are conservative in ways that are honestly debated: that one is a judgement, flagged).

2.2.5 Into the culinary brief

Per region and era, a "Peoples and speech" block: - the culture(s) here, with endonym gloss and pattern ("they call themselves 'the true people' (E2)"); - what each neighbour calls them and why ("'the mutes' to the coast folk (X3, little contact)"); - their lect: family, dialect-of, split date, MI to each neighbour both ways ("understand the river folk well (0.8); are understood less (0.6): they hear the river folk's traders more"); - who rules them, since when, in whose language; minorities and the lingua franca of the towns; - their gods' domains that touch food (harvest feast, a sea god's catch taboo); - food loan strata: "their word for barley came from the river folk ~4 kyr ago, by way of the hill folk"; - a suggested line set, as now, rule-based and overrulable: "a ruling minority's dishes are court food; the substrate's are village food" (a judgement line, but the classic pattern: English beef/cow, pork/swine).

This is what PW-856 wants: the culture, not the species, as the unit of the book.

5. Decisions for you

Each with options and my lean. L = language, N = names, P = polities, A = attitudes and gods.

L1. Is a culture a language? - A (lean): one lect per culture; languages are clusters by MI; families by the horizon. - B: one language per culture (simpler; but 111-130 "languages" per run where many would be dialects of each other). - C: a separate lect tree cut straight from the demes at a coarser scale.

L2. Where is the language line? MI between two lects at or above a threshold makes them one language. - A (lean): languages cut at Wichmann 2019's line (s ≈ 0.40, LDN 0.51, ~1.1-1.6 kyr apart). In the brief, use three bands for intelligibility: dialects (mi ≥ ~0.7), partly understood (0.4-0.7; 0.4 is Gooskens et al.'s workable threshold), separate (< 0.4). - B: cut languages by mi instead of s, so that exposure (a standard, a ruling lect) can merge "languages". This is closer to how people talk, but it makes the language count depend on politics.

L3. The rate. - A (lean): Holman's r = 0.72 per kyr, s0 0.92, with a lognormal multiplier per lect (sigma ~0.3, the fit's 29% scatter) and no era trend (lane 108's reading of Greenhill and Bromham agrees). An optional burst at a split is off by default. - B: rate tied to population (Bromham et al. 2015 found size changes gains and losses; the net effect is unclear). - C: A with the burst on.

L4. Time depth. - A (lean): the lect tree as deep as the demes (300 kyr). Families are visible only above a chance floor of inherited similarity, so the horizon emerges per family (~5-9 kyr at the sourced rate; your 6-10 kyr is the check). - B: start lects at ~20 kyr as PW-982 does (anything older is a set of unrelated isolate roots).

L5. Lects across species. - A (lean): no inherited relatedness across peoples; shift across peoples only under polities (Stage 1); a people born of a megafauna stem starts speaking the stem's lect (kobolds speak Draconic at their split, then drift). - B: the same, but megafauna-stem peoples start with their own speech. - C: no cross-people shift at all (each people keeps its own family forever).

L6. Dragons and giants. Cohesion already makes one culture per dragon colour. - A (lean): one lect per dragon culture, held together by cohesion; is "Draconic" one language across colours (MI high between colours) or a family (colours mutually unintelligible)? Lean: one language, colour dialects, MI ~0.8. - B: a family, colours unintelligible.

L7. The morphology scalar (PW-985) in Stage 0? It's one number per lect, nudged by speakers, adult L2 share and isolation, starting low. It is not "the language itself", but it's a step toward it. - A: in Stage 0, shown on the card only. - B (lean, given "none of the actual language"): wait until Stage 1 gives a real L2 share (from shift under states), which is what makes it meaningful.

N1. Gloss language. A (lean): English glosses ("the true people") in Stage 0; forms empty. B: no glosses, pattern codes only.

N2. Who gets names. A (lean): every living culture, and peoples-level rows from reputation.py merged in. B: only cultures above a size.

P1. Polities output-side or in the history? - A (lean): output-side v0 first (look at them), then in-history v1 for presence and war. - B: straight into the history (one build, but every tweak is a history re-run and the peoples' numbers move while we're still arguing about shapes).

P2. Tier gates. A (lean): chiefdom = chiefdoms + towns; state = kingship or law; empire = emergent (rules several cultures or peoples). B: state needs writing (the strict reading of "archaic state").

P3. War scope. A (lean): war between polities replaces the G and F proxies in K(W); in-fighting stays for stateless country and becomes fission inside polities; no war deaths (lane 101). B: also re-add war deaths with polity wars.

P4. The leader share. A (lean): after polities, report the commonest share and stop (D31 A stands); don't add polity knobs aimed at it. B: allow one polity dial ("how fragmented are the great peoples").

P5. Your empires. A (lean): emergent first, authored [[event]] kind = "polity" as a back-fill in Seven Meals if they don't appear. B: authored from the start.

A1. Attitudes as an extension of reputation.py, per culture pair. A (lean): yes. B: a new module with its own record.

A2. Gods. A (lean): domains only, from way of life and land; syncretism on merges; state cults under polities. B: also a rank (high god, local spirits) by polity tier (a sourced pattern exists but I didn't read it: flagged).

7. Where I'm unsure, said plainly

  • κ (the contact pull) has no literature value. It is the knob that decides how wide a dialect chain is. It will be tuned by eye on the "intelligibility from here" map against the checks in 2.0.2. That's a judgement and should be labelled one.
  • The MI curve is ours. No paper publishes a closed form. We fit it to European close relatives (the Germanic, Romance and Slavic test sets). Nothing says it transfers to dwarves. It's a placeholder curve with citations, not a law.
  • Exposure dominates real intelligibility (r ~0.9). So mi_ab will be driven mostly by E_ab, a judgement, and mi_inherent is the sourced half.
  • Frames are coarse. Cultures and lects split at frame resolution (0.5 kyr near the present); polities at the settlement step (100 years). Dynasties and short-lived states won't show until the history zoom (PW-908).
  • Culture count vs lect count. If you rule more cultures (PW-1251, PW-1254), you also get more lects. That's fine: languages are clusters, so the language count is robust to it.
  • Polities may come out too regular (Voronoi-ish states around towns). The circumscription and frontier terms are there to break that; if they don't, it's a sign the settlement ledger needs more geography (PW-1104).

  • t = (ln s - ln s0)/(2 ln r), s0 = 0.92, r = 0.72 per kyr, s = 1 - LDND; 52 calibration groups.
  • Corr(ln s, t) = -0.84; mean absolute error 29%.
  • Note: 0.72 is the retention constant, not a dialect threshold.
  • Wichmann 2019, "How to distinguish languages and dialects", Computational Linguistics 45(4):823-831. Full.
  • Language/dialect cut-off LDN 0.51 (resampling ~0.57); LDND = 1.00158 LDN + 0.08459.
  • The threshold corresponds to 1,075-1,635 years apart.
  • Table of pairs (above).
  • Lees 1953, "The basis of glottochronology", Language 29(2):113-127. Full.
  • Retention 0.8048 ± 0.0176 per millennium on the ~200-item list.
  • Swadesh's 86% for the 100-item list was seen only on secondary pages: not used.
  • Pagel, Atkinson & Meade 2007, Nature 449:717-720. Abstract. Word frequency explains ~50% of the variation in replacement rates (200 meanings, 87 Indo-European languages).
  • Greenhill et al. 2017, Bromham et al. 2015, Atkinson et al. 2008: not re-read this round. As cited by lane 108 in docs/review/emergent-cultures/design.md (Greenhill and Bromham read there, Atkinson a snippet only).
  • Gooskens 2007, J. Multilingual & Multicultural Development 28(6). Full.
  • Phonetic distance vs intelligibility: r = -0.64 overall, -0.80 Scandinavian.
  • Lexical: not significant (r 0.36), because Scandinavian non-cognates are only 0-3.6%.
  • Danes understand Swedish better than the reverse in that data.
  • Gooskens, van Heuven, Golubović, Schüppert, Swarte & Voigt 2017/18, "Mutual intelligibility between closely related languages in Europe", Int. J. Multilingualism. Full.
  • 16 languages, 1,833 listeners, cloze test.
  • Tree distance correlates with intelligibility: Germanic -0.67, Slavic -0.88.
  • Danish/Swedish with no exposure 44-56%; "40% correct could be set as a possible threshold".
  • Portuguese understand Spanish better than the reverse, even with minimal exposure.
  • Gooskens & van Heuven 2020, Linguistic Approaches to Bilingualism 10(3):351-379. Full.
  • 70 pairs. Exposure vs intelligibility r = 0.87-0.93: "the most important variable, overriding all other variables".
  • Attitudes add little.
  • Minimal-exposure listeners: Germanic lexical r = -0.95; stepwise R² 0.57 to 0.77 over all families.
  • No closed-form curve.
  • Nerbonne 2010, "Measuring the diffusion of linguistic change", Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 365:3821-3828. Full (through a page summariser).
  • Séguy's curve: aggregate distance grows with log geographic distance, r 0.41-0.62 over seven datasets; travel time beats straight-line distance.
  • About a quarter of the variance is geography.
  • A population (gravity) effect is weak or absent.
  • Abrams & Strogatz 2003, "Modelling the dynamics of language death", Nature 424:900. Full.
  • The model in 2.1.4; a = 1.31 ± 0.25 over 42 regions; status 0.26-0.46 in the fitted cases.
  • No stable coexistence without status feedback.
  • Nettle 1998, "Explaining global patterns of language diversity", J. Anthropological Archaeology 17:354-374. Full.
  • ln LANGS = 0.58 ln AREA + 0.23 MGS - 5.32 (43 tropical countries, r 0.66).
  • Regional fits 0.23-0.32 per growing month.
  • Gavin, Rangel et al. 2017, "Process-based modelling shows how climate and demography shape language diversity", Global Ecology & Biogeography 26:584-591. Full.
  • Australian simulation: carrying capacity a power law of rain.
  • A language starts at 10 people and buds a new one into an empty neighbouring cell when it reaches a maximum group size drawn from Binford's forager group sizes (not a fixed threshold).
  • Predicts 383-427 languages against 407 observed; r² 0.53-0.58 for richness.
  • Fitted constants are in the supplement (not read).
  • Relevant as an independent check that "a group buds a new language at a size limit" works, and as a hint that our deme sizes (PW-1015) matter for the lect count.
  • Amano et al. 2014, Proc. R. Soc. B 281:20141574. Abstract.
  • 25% of languages threatened.
  • Small ranges in tropical and arctic, rainy, rugged terrain; recent declines linked to economic growth.
  • Haspelmath & Tadmor (eds.) 2009, World Loanword Database, wold.clld.org. Data.
  • 41 languages; mean borrowed share 25.2% (median 24%), computed from the site.
  • Field and meaning scores as quoted in 2.0.5.
  • Tadmor's results chapter was not read.

Polities

  • Carneiro 2012, "The circumscription theory: a clarification, amplification, and reformulation", Social Evolution & History 11(2):5-30. Full.
  • The mechanism: hemmed-in growth leads to war, and war welds villages into chiefdoms, then states.
  • The first states "without exception" arose in circumscribed areas, but circumscription is "not absolutely essential".
  • Resource concentration works too, more slowly.
  • Social circumscription is "less tight", so states come "but it will take longer".
  • Chiefdoms spur neighbouring chiefdoms.
  • Carneiro 1970, Science 169:733-738: not read (paywall); used through the 2012 restatement.
  • Spencer 2010, "Territorial expansion and primary state formation", PNAS 107(16):7119-7126. Full.
  • A chiefdom's reach is "about one-half day of travel from the chiefly center, some 25–30 km by foot".
  • State institutions appear with expansion beyond a day's round trip. Six cases reached 50-250 km (Indus ~400 km).
  • Turchin 2009, "A theory for formation of large empires", J. Global History 4(2):191-217. Full.
  • 60+ mega-empires of 1 Mm² or more, 3000 BCE-1800 CE; over 90% in or next to the arid belt.
  • All but one of China's fifteen unifications came from the north.
  • Turchin, Currie, Turner & Gavrilets 2013, "War, space, and the evolution of Old World complex societies", PNAS 110(41):16384-16389. Full main text.
  • 100 km cells, 100-year steps.
  • Military tech seeded at the steppe edge: R² 0.65 against history; 0.48 without elevation; 0.17 with random seeding.
  • The supplement with the parameter values was not read.
  • Taagepera 1997, "Expansion and contraction patterns of large polities", International Studies Quarterly 41(3):475-504. Full.
  • Largest polity ≤ 1.1 Mm² before ~650 BC, never < 2.3 Mm² after; Achaemenid 5.5, Mongol 24, British 35 Mm².
  • Median duration 130 yr (78 polities); 16 lasted 300+ yr.
  • Duration vs rise time is weak (~D = 25 R^0.5).
  • Taagepera 1978/79: not read.
  • Taagepera & Nemčok 2024, More People, Fewer States: chapter summaries only.
  • Arbesman 2011, "The life-spans of empires", Historical Methods 44(3):127-129. Full. Exponential, λ = 0.46 per century, mean ~220 yr; 41 empires from Taagepera.
  • Currie, Greenhill, Gray, Hasegawa & Mace 2010, Nature 467:801-804. Abstract and summaries only.
  • Complexity rises and falls in small steps.
  • The model where falls may skip levels ranked second of six.
  • Rates not read.
  • Grinin 2003, "The early state and its analogues", Social Evolution & History 2(1):131-176. Full. Quotes Johnson & Earle 2000, which was not read directly:
  • simple chiefdom: thousands;
  • complex chiefdom: tens of thousands;
  • state: hundreds of thousands to millions. Smallest states are several thousand.
  • Currie & Mace 2009, "Political complexity predicts the spread of ethnolinguistic groups", PNAS 106(18):7339-7344. Full. As in 2.1.4.
  • Alexander, "Application of mathematical models to English secular cycles", Cliodynamics. Abstract. Secular cycles run 2-3 centuries. Turchin & Nefedov 2009 itself was not read.
